Lesson 1-2a · Bonding & Electronegativity

It's not a stick. It's an electrical attraction.

In middle school, chemical bonds are often drawn as solid lines or sticks connecting balls. In reality, a bond is a balance of electrical charges. Opposites attract, likes repel, and electrons are caught in a tug-of-war. By the end, you can use an element's position to predict how it shares electrons and what bond it forms.

Alignment
HS-PS1-2 Construct and revise an explanation for the outcome of a simple chemical reaction based on the outermost electron states of atoms, trends in the periodic table, and knowledge of the patterns of chemical properties.
Objective
You can explain a bond as an electrical force between charges. You can use the electronegativity difference to predict the bond type.
Scope
Main-group elements through krypton (element 36), plus the transition metals scandium to zinc for metallic bonding. Noble gases are left out because they do not bond.

Core Claims

  • Electrostatics: Chemical bonds are attractive electrostatic forces between opposite charges (positive nuclei pulling negative electrons).
  • Electronegativity Difference (ΔEN): Determines how valence electrons are distributed.
  • Covalent Sharing: Forms between nonmetals. Shared equally in nonpolar covalent (ΔEN < 0.5), shared unequally in polar covalent (0.5 ≤ ΔEN < 1.7).
  • Ionic Transfer: Metal transfers electrons to nonmetal (ΔEN ≥ 1.7), creating charged ions that attract.
  • Metallic Lattice: Metal atoms share delocalized valence electrons in a collective "sea of electrons."

01Origin of the force

What actually holds two atoms together?

A chemical bond is not a physical joint or glue. It is a push or pull between charges: an electrostatic force. Every atom has a positive nucleus (protons) and a cloud of negative electrons. When two atoms get close, they experience both attraction and repulsion:

Stage 1: The positive nucleus of each atom attracts the negative electron of the neighboring atom, pulling them together.
Stage 2: Squeezing the atoms too close causes positive nuclei and negative electrons to strongly repel, resisting collapse.
Stage 3: Inward attractive pull balances outward repulsive push exactly, settling the atoms into minimum potential energy.
The one idea to hold onto

A chemical bond is not a rigid stick. It is a balance of electrical forces. Attraction pulls the atoms together until repulsion pushes back. The bond sits at the distance where the two balance, which is the lowest-energy state.

Common mistake

Picturing a bond as a stick or a piece of glue. A bond is a push and pull between charges.

02Tug-of-war

How do different atoms share? It depends on their electronegativity.

Not all atoms pull on electrons with the same strength. An atom's power to attract shared electrons is called its electronegativity. Small, tight atoms with many protons (like fluorine) have very high electronegativity. Large atoms with shielded outer shells (like sodium) have very low electronegativity.

When two atoms bond, their electronegativity difference (ΔEN) governs electron distribution. This difference produces four distinct bond types:

↔
Balanced pull (ΔEN < 0.5)

Nonpolar Covalent

Equal sharing. Two nonmetals with similar strength (ΔEN < 0.5). Electrons spend equal time between the two nuclei. A nonpolar covalent bond has no charge separation.

→
Shifted pull (0.5 ≤ ΔEN < 1.7)

Polar Covalent

Unequal sharing. Two nonmetals with mismatched strength (0.5 ≤ ΔEN < 1.7). The hungrier atom pulls the electrons closer. It gets a partial negative charge (δ−), and the other atom gets a partial positive charge (δ+). This is a polar covalent bond.

⇒
Complete transfer (ΔEN ≥ 1.7)

Ionic

Electron transfer. A metal (low EN) and a nonmetal (high EN) with a huge strength mismatch (ΔEN ≥ 1.7). The nonmetal pulls the electron completely away. That forms positive and negative ions, which attract each other. This is an ionic bond.

∼
Delocalized sea (Low EN + Low EN)

Metallic

Delocalized sea. Two metal atoms (both low EN). Neither atom holds its outer electrons tightly, so they let go. The valence electrons float free among a grid of positive metal cores. This shared attraction is a metallic bond, and the free electrons form a sea of electrons.

Those four categories are not four separate rules. They are checkpoints along one continuous slide. Drag ΔEN below and watch the same electron cloud shift from centered, to lopsided, to gone.

The cloud drifts toward whichever atom pulls harder: past 1.7 it stops sharing and the charges go from δ to full.
Common mistake

Calling every bond above ΔEN = 1.7 ionic. Two nonmetals, such as H and F, still share electrons, so HF is polar covalent.

06Explain it to yourself

Explain how the electronegativities of two metal atoms create a "sea of electrons" instead of an ionic or covalent bond.

Writing it out forces your brain to organize the concept. Write your answer first, then peek and compare.

One way to say it

Metals have low electronegativity, meaning their nuclei exert a very weak grip on their valence electrons. When metal atoms meet, neither atom can lock its outer electrons in place, so there is no covalent sharing. Neither can strip electrons from its neighbor, so there is no ionic transfer. The valence electrons spread out into a sea of electrons. This mobile sea attracts the positive metal cores and holds them together.

Why This Matters

Dental crowns and joint replacements utilize custom bond properties. Biocompatible dental ceramics use ionic bonds that resist thermal shock and acids. In contrast, titanium implants use delocalized metallic electron seas to absorb stress and bond with bone.
