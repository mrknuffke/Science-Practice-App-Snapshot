Lesson 3-4a · Thermal Equilibrium & Heat Transfer

Your coffee always cools down. It will never spontaneously boil itself.

Leave a hot mug of coffee on your desk and it cools to room temperature. Now picture a lukewarm mug that pulls heat from the air and starts to boil while the room chills. Energy conservation allows that. It still never happens. Heat flows from warmer matter to cooler matter until the energy is spread evenly. The Second Law of Thermodynamics sets that direction.

Alignment
HS-PS3-4 Plan and conduct an investigation to provide evidence that the transfer of thermal energy when two components of different temperature are combined within a closed system results in a more uniform energy distribution among the components in the system (second law of thermodynamics).
Objective
You can plan an investigation that combines two components at different temperatures in a closed system. You can use the data to show the energy ends up more evenly shared. You can explain the direction of heat flow with particle collisions.
Scope
Heat flow in closed systems, collisions at a boundary, thermal equilibrium, and investigation planning. The statistics behind the Second Law are Enrichment.

Core Claims

  • The Second Law of Thermodynamics. In an isolated system, energy spreads out and does not gather again. Thermal energy flows from higher temperature to lower temperature. Entropy measures how spread out the energy is.
  • Thermal equilibrium. Two objects in contact reach the same temperature. Net heat transfer drops to zero because energy flows both ways at equal rates.
  • Collisions. When fast particles hit slow particles, the fast ones lose kinetic energy on average. The slow ones gain energy. The temperature difference shrinks.
  • Closed-system investigations. Mixing hot and cold components in an insulated container gives one temperature between the two starting values. That shows energy spreading out.

01Directionality in nature

The First Law allows it. The Second Law forbids it.

A glass of water at 20 °C sits on a table. Suddenly 500 J of thermal energy leaves the bottom half and gathers in the top half. The bottom half freezes at 0 °C. The top half heats to 40 °C.

Does this break the First Law of Thermodynamics? It does not. The total energy of the water stays constant (ΔE = 0).

Yet this will never happen. The Second Law of Thermodynamics sets the direction:

The Second Law of Thermodynamics

In any spontaneous process in an isolated system, the total entropy increases. Thermal energy flows from higher temperature to lower temperature until the temperature is the same everywhere. Entropy measures how spread out the energy is.

→

The First Law: how much

It answers “How much energy moved?” Energy cannot be created or destroyed. Energy lost by one component is gained by another (qsystem = −qsurroundings). It keeps the books. It does not say which way energy flows.

→

The Second Law: which way

It answers “Which way will energy flow?” Energy spreads out from hot regions until all connected matter has the same temperature.

Common mistake

Thinking energy conservation decides what happens. The First Law only checks the energy count. Many events keep the count and still never happen. The Second Law gives the direction.

02Submicroscopic origins

Why heat flows downhill: random collisions.

The direction of heat flow comes from collisions between particles. What happens when a hot block of iron touches a cool beaker of water?

A fast atom at the interface strikes a slow atom. Kinetic energy crosses the boundary. Billions of these collisions happen every microsecond:

  • Hot particles slow down on average, so Thot drops.
  • Cold particles speed up on average, so Tcold rises.
  • Net heat transfer continues until the average kinetic energy per particle is the same on both sides. That is thermal equilibrium. Collisions still happen, but energy passes both ways at equal rates.
Common mistake

Thinking particles stop colliding at equilibrium. They keep colliding. Energy passes both ways at equal rates, so the average speed stays the same on both sides.

Enrichment

Why the reverse never happens: counting the ways

Extra depth. The rest of the lesson works without it.

One collision could, by chance, move energy from a slow particle to a fast one. A real sample holds trillions of particles. For all of them to move energy the wrong way together is so unlikely that it never happens.

The reason is counting. There are far more ways for the particles to share the energy evenly than to keep it in one place. Each way is called a microstate. The even spread has the most microstates, so it is the state a system reaches. Entropy counts those ways.

06Explain it to yourself

A student watches an ice cube melt in a warm room and argues:

“The First Law says energy is conserved. Suppose a puddle took heat from itself, refroze, and warmed the room. The First Law would still hold. Why does this never happen?”

Write 3 to 4 sentences that answer the student. Use particle collisions and the Second Law of Thermodynamics.

Separate conservation (First Law) from direction (Second Law). Write your answer first, then compare it with the model answer.

One way to say it

The student is right that a refreezing puddle would satisfy the First Law. The Second Law of Thermodynamics forbids it, because energy spontaneously spreads out and does not gather again.

At the particle level, heat moves through collisions. Faster air particles hit slower water molecules and, on average, pass them kinetic energy. The water warms. For the puddle to freeze, the slow water molecules would have to pass energy to the faster air particles in nearly every collision. That does not happen in a real sample.

Scoring guide: 4 marks
  • Setup (1 mark): You set qlost = −qgained. Both samples are water, so c cancels.
  • Final temperature (1 mark): You solve for Tf and get 39.0 °C.
  • Evidence (1 mark): You say the two temperatures moved to one value, 39.0 °C, with no outside help.
  • Collisions (1 mark): You say fast particles lose kinetic energy and slow particles gain it until the averages match.
Why this matters

Heat engines get work from a temperature difference between a hot place and a cold place. Steam turbines, gas turbines, and car engines all work this way. The Second Law says no engine can turn all the heat into work. Some heat must flow to the cold side as waste. Engineers design power plants to use the largest temperature difference they can.
