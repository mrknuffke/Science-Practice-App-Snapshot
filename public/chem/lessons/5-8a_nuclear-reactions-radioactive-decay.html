Lesson 5-8a · Nuclear Reactions & Radioactive Decay

Chemical reactions rearrange atoms. Nuclear reactions turn one element into another.

In every chemical reaction so far, atoms kept their identity. A carbon atom went in as carbon and came out as carbon. Inside an unstable nucleus, that rule breaks. The nucleus changes, and the atom becomes a different element. This page shows how decay, fission and fusion change the nucleus, and how much energy they release.

Alignment

Objective
You can balance a nuclear equation by counting protons and neutrons. You can model alpha, beta and gamma decay. You can say what a half-life means and compare the energy of nuclear and chemical changes.
Scope
Nuclear notation. Alpha, beta and gamma decay. Half-life as half remaining. Fission and fusion as changes to the nucleus. The scale of the energy. The half-life equation, the energy of one fission and chain reactions are Enrichment.

Core Claims

  • Two totals stay the same. In every nuclear change, the total of protons plus neutrons (A) and the total of protons (Z) match on both sides.
  • Alpha decay. The nucleus ejects a helium-4 nucleus. A falls by 4 and Z falls by 2 (alpha decay).
  • Beta decay. A neutron changes into a proton and an electron leaves. Z rises by 1 and A stays the same (beta decay).
  • Gamma radiation. An excited nucleus sheds energy as a photon. A and Z do not change (gamma radiation).
  • Half-life. After each half-life, half of the sample remains.
  • Fission and fusion. Fission splits a heavy nucleus. Fusion joins light nuclei. Each releases about a million times more energy per atom than a chemical change.

01Count the particles

In a nuclear change, the element can change but two totals cannot.

In a chemical reaction, atoms rearrange their electrons and the nuclei stay untouched. In a nuclear reaction, the nucleus itself changes. We write a nucleus with nuclear notation:

AZX
  • Mass number (A): the number of protons plus neutrons.
  • Atomic number (Z): the number of protons.
The one idea to hold onto

A nuclear equation is balanced when two totals match on both sides. The total of A must match. The total of Z must match.

Common mistake

Thinking a nuclear equation keeps the same element on both sides, like a chemical one. In a nuclear change the element can change. Only the totals of A and Z stay the same.

02Three kinds of decay

Unstable nuclei decay by alpha, beta or gamma emission.

A nucleus with too many neutrons, or too much mass, is unstable. It decays by ejecting a particle or a photon.

→

1. Alpha decay: a helium-4 nucleus leaves

Heavy nuclei shed mass by ejecting an alpha particle, which is a helium-4 nucleus.

23892U → 23490Th + 42He

The mass number falls by 4. The atomic number falls by 2, so uranium becomes thorium.

→

2. Beta decay: a neutron becomes a proton

In a nucleus with too many neutrons, one neutron changes into a proton. The nucleus ejects an electron and an antineutrino.

146C → 147N + 0−1e + ν̄e

The mass number stays at 14. The atomic number rises by 1, so carbon becomes nitrogen.

→

3. Gamma radiation: a photon leaves

After alpha or beta decay, the new nucleus is often excited. It sheds the extra energy as a high-energy photon.

99m43Tc → 9943Tc + 00γ

The mass number and the atomic number do not change. The element stays the same.

Common mistake

Thinking the electron in beta decay was already inside the nucleus. A nucleus holds only protons and neutrons. The electron is made when a neutron changes into a proton.

03The clock in the nucleus

After each half-life, half of the sample remains.

Hold one atom of carbon-14. You cannot predict when it will decay. It might be in five seconds or in 20,000 years. Now hold a trillion carbon-14 atoms. About half of them decay in every 5,730 years.

The half-life is the time it takes for half of a sample to decay. Heat, pressure and chemical bonding do not change it, so half-lives work as clocks.

Common mistake

Thinking a sample is gone after two half-lives. After one half-life, half remain. After two, a quarter remain, not none. The sample never reaches zero in a few steps.

05Splitting and joining

Fission and fusion change the nucleus and release far more energy than chemical changes.

In nuclear fission, a heavy nucleus splits. A uranium-235 nucleus absorbs a neutron and splits into two smaller nuclei and three neutrons. Nuclear power plants use fission.

23592U + 10n → 14156Ba + 9236Kr + 3 10n

In nuclear fusion, light nuclei join. In the Sun, hydrogen nuclei join to make helium (Lesson 5-1a).

In both changes, the total number of protons and neutrons stays the same. In both, the nuclei change, so the elements change.

Common mistake

Thinking a nuclear change gives only a little more energy than a chemical one. One fission releases about 200 million eV. Forming a chemical bond releases 1 to 10 eV.

Enrichment

Equation, mass defect and chain reactions

Extra depth. The rest of the lesson works without it.

The half-life equation

After a time t, the number of nuclei left is N. Here N0 is the starting number and t1/2 is the half-life.

N = N0 (1/2)t/t1/2

After 3 half-lives, N = N0 × (1/2)3 = N0 / 8.

The energy of one fission

Weigh the particles before and after one fission. The products weigh a little less. The missing mass, the mass defect, becomes energy.

  • Before: uranium-235 (235.0439 u) + neutron (1.0087 u) = 236.0526 u
  • After: barium-141 + krypton-92 + 3 neutrons = 235.8665 u
  • Mass defect: 0.1861 u, which is about 173 MeV (2.8 × 10−11 J)
E = Δm · c2

A chain reaction

Each fission releases neutrons. A neutron can split another nucleus, which releases more neutrons. The number k is how many new fissions each fission causes. If k is above 1, the reaction grows. If k is 1, it holds steady. If k is below 1, it dies out.

A reactor holds k at 1 with control rods that absorb neutrons. Dropping every rod at once, called a SCRAM, pushes k far below 1 and stops the chain.

09Explain it to yourself

Heating a reactant speeds up a chemical reaction. But boiling carbon-14 in acid, or squeezing it to 100,000 atmospheres, does not change its half-life of 5,730 years.

Explain why radioactive decay is not affected by heat, pressure or chemical bonding.

Compare the energy of a chemical bond with the energy inside a nucleus. Write your answer first, then compare it with the model answer.

One way to say it

Chemical changes use the electrons of an atom. They involve 1 to 10 eV. Decay happens in the nucleus, where the energies are millions of eV.

Heat, pressure and bonding are tiny on that scale. The electron cloud also shields the nucleus from outside changes. So the half-life stays fixed.

Archaeologists find a wooden chest in an ancient burial site. They test a 1.00 g carbon sample from the wood. Living trees give 16.0 counts per minute (cpm) for each gram of carbon, from carbon-14. The sample from the chest gives 2.0 cpm. The half-life of carbon-14 (146C) is 5,730 years.

(a) Write the balanced nuclear equation for the beta decay of carbon-14. [1 mark]

(b) Find how many half-lives have passed since the tree was cut. Find the age of the chest. [2 marks]

(c) Explain why carbon-14 cannot date a dinosaur bone that is 68 million years old. Name an isotope that could. [1 mark]

Scoring guide: 4 marks
  • Equation (1 mark): You write 146C → 147N + 0−1e + ν̄e, with A and Z balanced.
  • Half-lives (1 mark): You halve 16.0 to 8.0, 4.0 and 2.0, so 3 half-lives have passed.
  • Age (1 mark): You find 3 × 5,730 = 17,190 years.
  • Limit (1 mark): You say almost no carbon-14 is left after 68 million years, and name uranium-238.
Why this matters

Doctors use decay to treat cancer. In targeted alpha therapy, an alpha-emitting isotope such as actinium-225 is attached to an antibody that finds cancer cells. An alpha particle travels less than 50 micrometers. It damages the tumor cell it sits in and spares the healthy tissue around it.
