Lesson 1-7c · Stoichiometry & BCA Tables

Predict yields, and find what runs out.

A balanced equation is an atomic recipe. In the lab you rarely mix the reactants in the exact ratio the recipe needs. One reactant runs out first and stops the reaction. A BCA (Before-Change-After) table tracks what is used up and what forms. Work in moles and coefficients, and you can predict how much product forms and what is left over.

Alignment
HS-PS1-7 Use mathematical representations to support the claim that atoms, and therefore mass, are conserved during a chemical reaction.
Objective
You can complete a BCA table for a reaction. You can name the limiting reactant. You can calculate the product yield and the excess left over.
Scope
Mole-to-mole stoichiometry, BCA tables, limiting and excess reactants, and the conservation of atoms.

Core Claims

  • Mole ratios: The coefficients of a balanced equation give the exact ratio of moles between reactants and products.
  • BCA tables: A Before-Change-After table tracks the moles of every substance. The Change row follows the coefficients.
  • Limiting reactant: The reactant that runs out first stops the reaction and sets the maximum yield. The other reactants are in excess.

01Proportion

Coefficients are scaling factors.

A sandwich recipe: 1 slice of cheese + 2 slices of bread → 1 sandwich. The ratio of cheese to bread is 1:2. With 5 slices of cheese and 6 slices of bread, you can make only 3 sandwiches. The bread runs out first and leaves 2 slices of cheese.

In chemistry, the coefficients of a balanced equation give the same kind of proportion, measured in moles. Consider the combustion of methane gas:

CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O

This equation shows that 1 mole of methane reacts with 2 moles of oxygen. It makes 1 mole of carbon dioxide and 2 moles of water. This is the mole ratio. Reacting 3 moles of CH4 takes 6 moles of O2. It makes 3 moles of CO2 and 6 moles of H2O.

Common mistake

Reading a coefficient as grams. The 2 in front of O2 means 2 moles of O2 for every 1 mole of CH4.

02Before-Change-After

The accounting table of chemistry.

To find a reaction yield, set up a BCA table (Before, Change, After). A BCA table tracks every substance at the same time:

  • Before (B): The starting moles of the reactants before the reaction begins. The products start at 0.
  • Change (C): The moles used up (negative) or made (positive). The values in this row must scale in the exact ratio of the equation's coefficients.
  • After (A): The moles left when the reaction ends (Before + Change).

Here is a balanced BCA table. 2.0 moles of CH4 and 4.0 moles of O2 are mixed in the exact 1:2 ratio. Both reactants are fully consumed:

The Change row is locked to the 1:2:1:2 coefficient ratio: reactants fall and products rise in that exact proportion.
Row CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O
Before (B) 2.0 mol 4.0 mol 0 mol 0 mol
Change (C) −2.0 mol −4.0 mol +2.0 mol +4.0 mol
After (A) 0 mol 0 mol 2.0 mol 4.0 mol
Before + Change = After. 2.0 mol CH4 and 4.0 mol O2 are in the exact 1:2 ratio. Both reactants reach zero together, and nothing is left over.

Notice that the Change row values (−2.0, −4.0, +2.0, +4.0) match the 1:2:1:2 coefficient ratio of the equation. Reactants are consumed and products are built in locked step.

Common mistake

Filling the Change row with equal numbers. The row follows the coefficients: −2.0 mol CH4 goes with −4.0 mol O2, not −2.0 mol.

03What runs out?

The limiting reactant sets the limit.

What if you mix 2.0 moles of CH4 with 5.0 moles of O2? There is not enough methane to react with all of the oxygen. Methane runs out first.

The reactant that is completely consumed first is the limiting reactant. It limits how much product can form. The reactant that remains after the reaction halts is the excess reactant.

To find the limiting reactant with a BCA table, test which reactant would hit 0 first if it were fully consumed. The one with the smaller Change row multiplier is the limiting reactant. When it hits 0 in the After row, the reaction stops. The excess reactant stays in the container unchanged.

Common mistake

Picking the reactant with the fewest moles. In Question 1 below, 3.0 mol CH4 is less than 4.0 mol O2, yet O2 runs out first, because each CH4 needs 2 O2.

03bThe s'mores mistake

The fewest pieces is not always the limiting reactant.

Many students think the reactant with the smallest amount always runs out first. The s'mores test shows why that is wrong. Each s'more needs 2 graham crackers + 1 marshmallow + 1 chocolate square. Having the fewest marshmallows does not make them limiting. You have to check the recipe ratio.

From s'mores to chemistry: the BCA ratio method

Every limiting reactant problem follows the same logic. Divide each reactant's available moles by its coefficient. The smallest quotient identifies the limiting reactant.

General rule

The limiting reactant has the smallest value of: available moles ÷ coefficient.

The s'mores check: crackers ÷ 2 = 5, marshmallows ÷ 1 = 6, chocolate ÷ 1 = 8. The crackers are limiting, because 5 is the smallest.

07Explain it to yourself

Explain why the Change row must match the equation's coefficients, even when the Before amounts differ.

Writing it out forces your brain to organize the idea. Write your answer first, then peek and compare.

One way to say it

The Before row holds whatever amounts you happened to mix. But molecules react in fixed proportions set by the balanced equation. Each CH4 molecule needs 2 O2 molecules and makes 1 CO2 and 2 H2O. So the particles used up and made always follow the coefficient ratio. The Change row tracks that, so it must match the coefficients exactly.

Gen Chem · HS-PS1-7 · constructed response[4 marks]

A mixture holds 1.50 moles of methane (CH4) and 2.00 moles of oxygen (O2). They react: CH4 + 2 O2 → CO2 + 2 H2O. Name the limiting reactant. Calculate the maximum moles of H2O formed. Find the moles of excess reactant left over. Show all work.

Scoring guide: 4 marks
  • Setup (1 mark): You list the starting amounts: 1.50 mol CH4, 2.00 mol O2 and 0 mol H2O.
  • Limiting reactant (1 mark): You name O2 and show that 1.50 mol CH4 needs 3.00 mol O2.
  • Yield (1 mark): You find 2.00 mol H2O, from 2.00 mol O2 in a 2:2 ratio.
  • Excess (1 mark): You find 0.50 mol CH4 left over (1.50 mol minus 1.00 mol reacted).
Why This Matters

Fertilizer depends on stoichiometry. The Haber-Bosch process makes ammonia (N₂ + 3 H₂ → 2 NH₃), and ammonia fertilizer feeds half the world's population. Mixing the reactants in the exact 1:3 ratio wastes none of them.
