Lesson 1-1b · Periodic Trends & Reactivity

One metal explodes in water. One sits in your wiring doing nothing.

Drop a pea-sized lump of sodium into water and it skitters, hisses, and bursts into flame. Drop in a copper penny and… nothing. Same periodic table, different addresses. By the end of this page, you can predict that difference from an element's position alone.

Alignment
HS-PS1-1Use the periodic table as a model to predict the relative properties of elements based on the patterns of electrons in the outermost energy level of atoms.
Objective
You can predict and compare the atomic radius, ionization energy, and electronegativity of two elements from their positions. You can explain how reactive each one is, and why.
Scope
Main-group elements through krypton (element 36). Transition metals come later, because their trends are messier. The numbers in the tables are supporting data. The target is the relative trend: higher or lower, not the exact value.

Core Claims

  • Atomic Radius: Decreases left to right, because more protons pull the same shell tighter. Increases top to bottom, because each row adds a new electron shell.
  • Ionization Energy (IE): Energy to remove an electron. Increases left-to-right (tighter nuclear grip); decreases top-to-bottom (outer electrons farther and shielded).
  • Electronegativity (EN): Atom's power to pull shared electrons. Follows the same trends as IE (excluding noble gases).
  • Reactivity: Metals react by losing electrons (highest bottom-left); nonmetals react by gaining electrons (highest top-right).

01Why position predicts anything at all

An element's address on the table tells you how it behaves.

Every periodic trend comes down to one tug-of-war over the outermost electrons. The positive nucleus pulls them in. Distance and the layers of inner electrons weaken that pull. Two things change the strength of the tug-of-war. They line up with the two directions you can move on the table.

→

Across a period

Each step right adds one proton, but the new electron joins the same shell. More pull at the same distance: the grip tightens and the atom shrinks.

↓

Down a group

Each step down adds a whole new shell. The outer electrons sit farther out, and the inner electrons shield them. The grip loosens and the atom grows.

The one idea to hold onto

Tighter grip on the outer electrons → smaller atom, harder to remove an electron, stronger pull on shared electrons. Looser grip → all of that, reversed. Everything below is this idea from a different angle.

Common mistake

Thinking more protons and electrons make a bigger atom. Across a period the new electrons join the same shell, and the extra protons pull that shell in.

05The trend that flips

Reactivity isn't one arrow. It depends on whether the atom wants to lose or wants to gain electrons.

Atomic radius, ionization energy (IE), and electronegativity (EN) each run in one consistent direction. Reactivity does not, and that is what trips people up. Stop asking "is it reactive?" Start asking "reactive how?"

Metals lose electrons. Most reactive where that is easiest (low ionization energy), so down a group and to the left. The alkali metals (Li → Na → K) are the wild ones.

Nonmetals gain electrons. Most reactive where the pull is strongest (high electronegativity), so up a group and to the right. Fluorine is the champion.

Noble gases barely react. A full outer shell means no reason to lose or gain. The far-right column stays calm.

Common mistake

Reading reactivity as one trend. Metals get more reactive toward the bottom left. Nonmetals get more reactive toward the top right.

      Magnesium (Mg + 2 HCl)Copper (Cu + HCl)
      ObservationVigorous fizzing and gas evolution (H2 gas).No reaction. The strip stays shiny.
      ReasoningLow ionization energy. Mg easily sheds 2 valence electrons to H+ ions: Mg(s) + 2 H+(aq) → Mg2+(aq) + H2(g).High ionization energy. Copper holds its valence electrons tightly, and H+ ions cannot pull electrons away from Cu.
      08From position to the hazard label

      This is why you can read a hazard off the table, before you ever see the bottle.

      Back to where we started, and now the address explains it. At the particulate level, sodium loosely holds its single outer electron. Its low ionization energy allows that electron to leave with minimal energy input. That eagerness to lose an electron is what makes sodium tear into water. Copper is a transition metal, outside our main-group scope. It holds its electrons far more tightly, so it just sits there.

      Bottom-left metals → flagged reactive or water-reactive. Top-right nonmetals (fluorine, chlorine) → corrosive, aggressive oxidizers. Far-right noble gases → inert and safe. Position first, label second.

      For example, you need to know the exact hazard profile for elemental sodium (Na). Sodium reacts violently with water and carries two GHS pictograms. The Flame marks extreme water reactivity and flammable hydrogen release. The Corrosion symbol marks generated sodium hydroxide, a caustic base causing severe tissue damage.

      The limit: say it out loud

      Position predicts intrinsic reactivity: what an element tends to do. It does not tell you concentration, quantity, or context. A trace behind glass can be safe; a drum of a "mild" element may not be. The table predicts the tendency, not the situation.

      10Explain it to yourself

      Ionization energy increases across a period but decreases down a group. In your own words, why do those two directions go opposite ways?

      No one is grading this. Writing the reasoning out moves it from "I recognize it" to "I can produce it." Then compare with a model answer.

      One way to say it

      Ionization energy is the energy needed to remove an outer electron, so it tracks how tightly that electron is held. Across a period, protons pile up in the same shell. The pull tightens, the electron is harder to remove, and ionization energy rises. Down a group, the outer electron sits in a farther, shielded shell. The nuclear pull loosens, the electron leaves more easily, and ionization energy drops. Same tug-of-war, two different things changing.

      Why This Matters

      Lithium battery technology relies on periodic trends. Lithium's extremely low ionization energy and small atomic radius make it incredibly eager to shed its single valence electron. This high electron-releasing drive is what makes lithium the optimal anode material for the lightweight, high-energy rechargeable batteries in our smartphones.
