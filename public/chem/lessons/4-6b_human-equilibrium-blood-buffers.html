Lesson 4-6b · Human Equilibrium & Blood Buffers

A 400-meter sprint pours acid into your blood. Your blood barely notices.

During a 400-meter sprint, your muscles pour lactic acid into your blood. Your blood has to stay almost exactly as acidic as it was, or the enzymes in your heart and brain stop working. Part of the defense is an equilibrium that links your blood to your lungs. This page shows how breathing faster or slower shifts it.

Core Claims

  • One equilibrium. Carbon dioxide dissolves in blood and reacts with water: CO2 + H2O ⇌ H+ + HCO3−. More CO2 means more H+, and H+ makes the blood more acidic.
  • Breathing faster. You breathe out more CO2. Removing a reactant shifts the equilibrium left. The shift uses up H+, and the blood becomes less acidic (alkalosis).
  • Breathing slower. CO2 builds up. The extra reactant shifts the equilibrium right. The shift makes H+, and the blood becomes more acidic (acidosis).
  • Paper bag. Rebreathing your own CO2 raises the CO2 in the blood and shifts the equilibrium back to the right.

01An equilibrium in your blood

One equilibrium links your blood to your lungs.

Your cells make carbon dioxide all the time. It dissolves in your blood. There it reacts with water and makes a little acid.

CO2(aq) + H2O(l) ⇌ H+(aq) + HCO3−(aq)

This is a reversible reaction at dynamic equilibrium, like the one in the Haber process. More CO2 in the blood means more H+ ions, and H+ is what makes a liquid acidic. Your lungs control how much CO2 stays in your blood. Every breath out removes some.

Common mistake

Thinking CO2 is only a waste gas with no effect on the blood. Dissolved CO2 makes H+, so the amount of CO2 in the blood changes how acidic the blood is.

02Faster and slower breathing

Fast breathing, slow breathing, and Le Chatelier.

When you change how fast you breathe, you change the CO2 in your blood. By Le Chatelier’s principle, the equilibrium shifts to work against that change.

→

Breathing faster: the blood becomes less acidic

During a panic attack or at high altitude, fast, deep breathing removes CO2 faster than your cells make it. CO2 is a reactant, so removing it shifts the equilibrium left (←).
H+(aq) + HCO3−(aq) → CO2(aq) + H2O(l)
The shift uses up H+, so the blood becomes less acidic. Doctors call this alkalosis. The person feels tingling and lightheaded, and muscles can spasm.
Treatment: Breathing into a paper bag makes the person rebreathe their own CO2. The CO2 in the blood rises and the equilibrium shifts right (→). The H+ comes back.

→

Breathing slower: the blood becomes more acidic

During a severe asthma attack, choking or an opioid overdose, breathing is too shallow to remove CO2. The CO2 builds up in the blood. The extra reactant shifts the equilibrium right (→).
CO2(aq) + H2O(l) → H+(aq) + HCO3−(aq)
The shift makes H+, so the blood becomes more acidic. Doctors call this acidosis. Without a ventilator or emergency oxygen, severe acidosis weakens the heart and can cause a coma.

Common mistake

Thinking faster breathing makes the blood more acidic because you take in more air. Faster breathing blows off CO2, and CO2 makes acid. Less CO2 means less H+.

A buffer: a weak acid and its conjugate base

Add 1.0 milliliter of 1.0 M hydrochloric acid (about 20 drops) to 1.0 liter of pure water. The pH falls from 7.0 to 3.0. That is a 10,000-fold increase in H+. Add the same acid to 1.0 liter of human blood, with breathing keeping CO2 steady, and the pH changes by less than 0.02. A buffer makes the difference.

A buffer holds large amounts of both a weak acid (a proton donor, HA) and its conjugate base (a proton acceptor, A−).

→

When strong acid is added

The conjugate base binds the added H+ and makes weak acid:
A−(aq) + H+(aq) → HA(aq)
The free H+ turns into undissociated weak acid, so the pH barely changes.

→

When strong base is added

The weak acid gives up H+ to neutralize the added hydroxide:
HA(aq) + OH−(aq) → A−(aq) + H2O(l)
The OH− turns into water and conjugate base, so the pH barely changes.

The pH window and the bicarbonate chain

Human blood plasma stays between pH 7.35 and 7.45. Below 7.35 is acidosis, which slows the central nervous system. Above 7.45 is alkalosis, which makes nerves overexcitable and muscles spasm. A pH below 6.8 or above 7.8 is quickly fatal.

The bicarbonate buffer is the main defense. It links the lungs, the blood and the kidneys in one chain of equilibria.

One way to say it

Fast breathing removes CO2 from the blood. CO2 is a reactant in CO2 + H2O ⇌ H+ + HCO3−, so removing it shifts the equilibrium left. The shift uses up H+. The blood becomes less acidic, and that causes the tingling.

Breathing into a bag makes the person rebreathe their own CO2. The CO2 in the blood rises and the equilibrium shifts right. The H+ comes back, and the blood returns toward its normal acidity.

Why this matters

Patients with end-stage kidney failure cannot remove acid or rebuild the bicarbonate in their blood. Without treatment, the acid build-up is quickly fatal. A hemodialysis machine works as an artificial kidney. It passes blood across a thin membrane that is bathed in a fluid rich in bicarbonate (32 to 35 mM). Bicarbonate moves into the blood and acid wastes move out. The treatment restores the balance three times a week.
