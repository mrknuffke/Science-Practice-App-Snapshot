Lesson 3-1a · Temperature, Heat & Specific Heat

A sparkler at 1,000 °C won’t burn you. A warm bath will.

A sparkler spark glows at over 1,000 °C and barely stings your skin. A bath at 60 °C scalds you in seconds. Temperature alone does not decide how much energy reaches you. This page separates temperature, thermal energy, and heat, then uses q = mcΔT to calculate a transfer.

Alignment
HS-PS3-1 Create a computational model to calculate the change in the energy of one component in a system when the change in energy of the other component(s) and energy flows in and out of the system are known.
Objective
You can tell temperature, thermal energy, and heat apart. You can use q = mcΔT to calculate the energy a sample gains or loses.
Scope
Thermal energy, specific heat, and warming or cooling in a single phase. Phase changes come in 3-1b.

Core Claims

  • Temperature: The average kinetic energy of the particles in a sample. It does not depend on how much material you have.
  • Thermal energy (Eth): The total kinetic energy of all the particles. It grows with the mass of the sample.
  • Heat (q): Thermal energy moving across a boundary because of a temperature difference. Matter stores thermal energy, never heat.
  • Specific heat (c): The energy that raises 1 g of a substance by 1 °C. Water has a high c. Metals have a low c.
  • The model: q = m × c × ΔT gives the energy transferred while a substance warms or cools in one phase.

01Particle motion and total energy

Temperature is an average. Thermal energy is a total.

All matter is made of particles in constant, random motion. Heat a sample and its particles move faster and collide harder. Everyday speech blurs two ideas here. Keep them apart.

°C

Temperature

Temperature measures the average kinetic energy (KEavg) of the particles in a sample. It is an intensive property: it does not depend on how much material you have. A droplet of boiling water and a cauldron of boiling water are both at 100 °C. Their particles move at the same average speed.

Eth

Thermal energy

Thermal energy is the total kinetic energy of all the particles together. It is an extensive property: it grows with mass. The cauldron holds thousands of times more particles than the droplet. It holds thousands of times more thermal energy.

Now the sparkler makes sense. A spark is at about 1,200 °C, so its few iron and aluminum atoms vibrate at high speed. But its mass is under 0.0001 g. Its total thermal energy is tiny, measured in millijoules. The spark cools on your skin before it passes on enough energy to burn.

Common mistake

Thinking the hotter sample always holds more thermal energy. Temperature tells you how fast the particles move. It does not tell you how many particles there are. A bathtub at 50 °C holds far more thermal energy than a spark at 1,200 °C.

03Energy in transit

You cannot “possess” heat.

People say an object “holds heat” or “loses its heat.” In physical science that is wrong. Matter holds thermal energy. It does not hold heat.

Think of thermal energy as money in your bank account. Heat (symbol q) is a wire transfer. You never carry a wire transfer in your wallet. A wire transfer is the act of moving money between accounts. In the same way, heat is energy in the act of moving across a boundary because of a temperature difference.

Why heat flows from hot to cold

A hot object touches a cold object. Fast particles at the boundary hit slower particles. In each collision, the fast particle slows a little and the slow particle speeds up. Energy moves from the hot object to the cold one. It keeps moving until both have the same average particle speed. That state is thermal equilibrium.

Common mistake

Saying a cold object “gives off cold.” Cold is not something that moves. Thermal energy moves from the hotter object to the cooler one. A cool object only has less energy to give.

04How much energy a substance needs

Why does the pool stay cool while the pavement scorches?

Walk barefoot on a sunny afternoon in Singapore. The concrete path burns your soles. The pool beside it feels cool. Both took in the same sunlight for the same four hours. Why did the concrete get so hot while the water barely warmed by a degree?

The answer is specific heat capacity (symbol c), or specific heat for short. Specific heat measures how strongly a substance resists a change in temperature.

Definition: specific heat capacity (c)

The heat needed to raise the temperature of 1 gram of a substance by 1 °C (or 1 K). The unit is J/(g·°C).

A substance with a low specific heat needs little energy to speed up its particles. It warms fast and cools fast. A substance with a high specific heat acts like a thermal sponge. It absorbs a lot of energy while its temperature rises only a little.

Specific heat of common substances
Substance Specific heat c (J/(g·°C)) What it means
Liquid water (H2O) 4.18 Highest in the table. It moderates coastal climates and blood temperature.
Ethanol 2.44 Warms nearly twice as fast as water for the same heat input.
Concrete or granite 0.79 – 0.88 Scorches in direct sunlight because it needs less than a quarter of the heat that water needs.
Aluminum (Al) 0.897 Heats fast. Cookware and heat sinks use it.
Iron (Fe) 0.449 Cast iron pans heat quickly and hold thermal energy.
Copper (Cu) 0.385 Lowest in the table. It warms with little heat input.
Common mistake

Thinking a high specific heat means a substance gets hot. It means the opposite. Water has the highest c in the table, so it takes the most energy to warm. That is why the pool stays cool.

05The model in one line

Calculate the energy: q = mcΔT

Three things set how much heat enters or leaves a sample: its mass, its specific heat, and its temperature change. Multiply them.

q = m × c × ΔT
  • q is the heat (J or kJ).
  • m is the mass (g).
  • c is the specific heat (J/(g·°C)).
  • ΔT is Tfinal − Tinitial (°C).

Watch the signs. Energy entering the system is positive. Energy leaving the system is negative.

  • The substance warms up. Tfinal > Tinitial, so ΔT is positive and q is positive (+q). The substance absorbs energy.
  • The substance cools down. Tfinal < Tinitial, so ΔT is negative and q is negative (−q). The substance releases energy to its surroundings.
Common mistake

Dropping the sign. A cooling sample has a negative ΔT, so q is negative. A positive q for a cooling sample means you subtracted in the wrong order.

08Explain it to yourself

A classmate says: “A cup of boiling water contains much more heat than an iceberg, because 100 °C is much hotter than 0 °C.”

Critique the statement in 2 to 3 sentences. Say what is wrong with the word “heat.” Then compare the energy stored in the two bodies.

One way to say it

The classmate is wrong in two ways. First, matter stores thermal energy, never heat. Heat is energy in transit across a boundary. Second, the boiling water has the higher temperature, so its particles move faster on average. But the iceberg has far more mass, so it has far more particles. Thermal energy adds up over all the particles. The iceberg holds far more thermal energy than a 250 g cup of boiling water.

Why this matters

Oceans cover over 70% of Earth and hold about 1.4 × 1021 kg of water. Water has a high specific heat (4.18 J/(g·°C)), so oceans absorb sunlight with little temperature rise. They carry that energy toward the poles and moderate the global climate. Without that buffer, day and night and summer and winter would swing as widely as they do on Mars.
