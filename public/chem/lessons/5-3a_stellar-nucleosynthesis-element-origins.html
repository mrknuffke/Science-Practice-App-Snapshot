Lesson 5-3a · Stellar Nucleosynthesis & Element Origins

The Big Bang made almost nothing but hydrogen and helium. The carbon, oxygen and iron in your body were made in stars.

When the early universe cooled, it held hydrogen, helium and a trace of lithium. It held no carbon, no oxygen and no iron. Without them there could be no rock, no planets and no life. Stars built these elements by fusing light nuclei into heavier ones. This page shows which elements form where, and how a star’s mass and age decide what it makes.

Scope
Hydrogen and helium from the Big Bang. Elements up to iron inside stars. Heavier elements from explosions and collisions. Shell-by-shell burning, binding energy and neutron capture are Enrichment.

Core Claims

  • The Big Bang. It made hydrogen, helium and a trace of lithium.
  • Inside stars. Fusion builds elements from helium up to iron (stellar nucleosynthesis). A star’s mass sets how far it goes.
  • Explosions and collisions. Elements heavier than iron form in a supernova and when neutron stars collide.
  • Recycling. Dying stars scatter new elements into space. New stars and planets form from that gas.

01Three places

Three kinds of events made all the elements.

The Big Bang made hydrogen, helium and a trace of lithium. Nothing heavier formed.

Inside stars, fusion joins light nuclei into heavier ones. This makes every element up to and including iron. The process is stellar nucleosynthesis.

Elements heavier than iron form in two violent events. A massive star can explode in a supernova. Two neutron stars can also collide. Both events scatter the new elements across space.

Common mistake

Thinking stars can fuse their way to every element, even gold. Fusion inside stars stops at iron. Gold needs a supernova or a collision of neutron stars.

02Mass and age

A star’s mass and age decide which elements it makes.

A star makes elements by fusing nuclei in its core. How far it goes depends on its mass, because mass sets how hot the core gets.

→

A star like the Sun

The Sun fuses hydrogen into helium for about 10 billion years. Only when the hydrogen runs out does its core fuse helium into carbon and oxygen. It never gets hot enough to build anything heavier.

→

A massive star

A star with more than 8 times the Sun’s mass is squeezed harder. Its core gets hotter. When one fuel runs out, the core fuses the ash of the last reaction. Late in its life, the star has built elements up to iron.

Age matters too. A massive star begins with hydrogen only. It makes heavier elements one at a time as it ages. It lives fast and dies young.

Common mistake

Thinking every star makes iron. Only massive stars get hot enough. A star like the Sun stops long before iron.

03The heaviest elements

Explosions and collisions make the heaviest elements and scatter them.

When a massive star runs out of fuel, its core collapses. The star then explodes in a supernova. The blast makes elements such as gold, platinum and uranium. Two colliding neutron stars make them too.

These events also throw every new element into space. The gas drifts together and forms new stars and planets. The iron in your blood was made in a star that died before the Sun formed.

Common mistake

Thinking an exploding star only destroys things. A supernova is how new elements reach space. Without one, there would be no gold and no rocky planets.

Shell-by-shell burning

In a star with more than 8 times the Sun’s mass, each fuel burns in turn. When a fuel runs out, the core shrinks and heats up. The ash of the last reaction then becomes the next fuel. The star ends up with burning shells like the layers of an onion.

Use your evidence

The iron peak

Why can the star not fuse iron too? The answer is in the binding energy per nucleon (Figure 5.5). Joining nuclei lighter than iron releases energy. Iron-56 sits at the peak of the curve, at 8.79 MeV per nucleon. Fusing iron takes in energy instead of releasing it.

When the core holds about 1.4 solar masses of iron, fusion stops. Iron absorbs heat instead of releasing it. The core cools, and its outward pressure vanishes. In less than a quarter of a second, gravity pulls the star inward at 70,000 kilometers a second, 23% of the speed of light. The collapse ends in a supernova.

Neutron capture

Fusion past iron is impossible, so how did gold and uranium form? Neutrons have no electric charge. They can enter a nucleus without being repelled. Inside the nucleus, a neutron can change into a proton. This moves the element up the periodic table.

→

The s-process: slow capture

It happens in old, pulsing giant stars over thousands of years. An iron nucleus catches a stray neutron every few hundred years. It has time to change before the next one arrives. This builds strontium, barium and lead.

→

The r-process: rapid capture

It happens in a supernova and when neutron stars collide. Trillions of neutrons fill each cubic centimeter. A nucleus catches dozens of them in a microsecond. The heavy nuclei then change into gold, platinum and uranium.

Explain it

Use the binding energy curve to explain why a massive star cannot keep fusing once its core is iron.

One way to say it

Below iron, joining nuclei raises the binding energy per nucleon, so fusion releases energy. Iron-56 has the highest value. Fusing iron lowers it, so fusion takes in energy.

The core then loses its outward pressure. Gravity wins, and the core collapses.

06Explain it to yourself

A student says: “The Sun has fused for 4.6 billion years. Given enough time, any star makes any element, even gold.”

Explain what is wrong with this idea.

Think about what sets which elements a star can make. Write your answer first, then compare it with the model answer.

One way to say it

The student is wrong. Time does not heat a core. The Sun’s mass sets its core temperature, and that temperature is too low to build iron or gold.

Only massive stars get hot enough to build elements up to iron. Gold is heavier than iron, so no star makes it by fusion. It forms in a supernova or when neutron stars collide.

Why this matters

In August 2017, the LIGO and Virgo detectors sensed ripples in space from two neutron stars colliding 130 million light-years away. Telescopes watched the glowing debris. Its light showed that the collision made huge amounts of heavy elements, including gold and platinum. Wedding rings and electronics hold atoms made in events like it.
