Lesson 4-6a · Dynamic Equilibrium & Le Chatelier’s Principle

Nearly half the nitrogen in your body came from a reaction that never finishes.

Nearly half the nitrogen atoms in your proteins were fixed from the air by the Haber-Bosch process: N2(g) + 3 H2(g) ⇌ 2 NH3(g) + heat. As soon as ammonia forms, collisions between ammonia molecules break some of it apart again. In a closed vessel, the forward and reverse reactions settle at the same rate. Engineers then push on that balance with temperature, pressure and product removal, and make millions of tons of ammonia.

Alignment
HS-PS1-6 Refine the design of a chemical system by specifying a change in conditions that would produce increased amounts of products at equilibrium.
Objective
You can describe equilibrium as two reactions that run at the same rate. You can predict which way a system shifts when you change one condition. You can use that to make more product.
Scope
Reversible reactions, and why an equilibrium looks steady. One change at a time: more reactant, less product, a new temperature, or a new pressure. Why the Haber process is a compromise.

Core Claims

  • Dynamic equilibrium. In a closed system, a reversible reaction reaches dynamic equilibrium when the forward and reverse rates are equal and not zero.
  • Steady, yet busy. At equilibrium the amounts of reactants and products stay constant, even though both reactions keep running at the particle level.
  • Le Chatelier’s principle. When a change disturbs a system at equilibrium, the system shifts to work against the change. The changes are concentration, temperature and pressure.
  • Concentration. Adding reactant or removing product makes the forward rate larger than the reverse rate. The system shifts right, toward more products.
  • Temperature. Treat heat as a product of an exothermic reaction. Heating shifts the system left. Cooling shifts it right.
  • Pressure. Compressing a gas mixture shifts the system toward the side with fewer moles of gas.

Equal rates, constant amounts

01Two reactions at the same rate

Running on a molecular treadmill: the rate balance.

Imagine walking forward on a treadmill. You walk at 4.0 miles per hour and the belt moves backward at 4.0 miles per hour. You stay in the same place. A person across the room sees nothing change. Your legs are working hard, though. If you or the belt speeds up, your position shifts at once.

A reversible reaction at dynamic equilibrium works the same way.

The one idea to hold onto

At equilibrium the forward and reverse rates are equal, and they are not zero. The amounts stay constant, but they are not always equal.

Common mistake

Thinking equilibrium means the reactions have stopped, or that reactants and products must be equal in amount. Both reactions keep running at the same rate. A mixture at equilibrium can hold 99% products or 99% reactants.

02How a system pushes back

Le Chatelier’s Principle: nature’s chemical pushback.

In 1884 the French chemist Henri Louis Le Chatelier described how a system at equilibrium answers a change. His idea is called Le Chatelier’s Principle.

Le Chatelier’s Principle

When a change disturbs a system at equilibrium, the system shifts its reaction rates in the direction that works against the change.

→

1. Changing a concentration

Add reactant. More reactant means more collisions between reactant molecules. The forward rate rises above the reverse rate, and the system shifts right (→) toward products.
Remove product. With less product around, fewer product molecules collide, so the reverse rate drops. The forward rate is now larger, and the system keeps shifting right (→) to replace the missing product.

→

2. Changing the temperature

To predict a temperature shift, treat heat as a reactant or a product.
• Exothermic: Reactants ⇌ Products + heat. Heating adds heat, so the system shifts left (←) to use it up. Cooling shifts it right (→).
• Endothermic: Reactants + heat ⇌ Products. Heating shifts it right (→). Cooling shifts it left (←).
Temperature is the only change that alters the value of the equilibrium constant.

→

3. Changing the pressure or volume of a gas

Compressing the vessel raises the pressure and packs the gas particles closer. The system works against this by shifting toward the side with fewer moles of gas.
In the Haber process, N2(g) + 3 H2(g) is 4 moles of gas and 2 NH3(g) is 2 moles. Raising the pressure shifts the system right (→), toward the 2-mole side.

Common mistake

Thinking the system shifts toward the side that just grew. The system works against the change. Adding reactant shifts it right, toward the products.

03An engineering compromise

The Haber-Bosch dilemma: cold favors yield, heat favors speed.

Here is the reaction that feeds the world:

N2(g) + 3 H2(g) ⇌ 2 NH3(g) + 92.4 kJ   (ΔH = −92.4 kJ/mol)

An engineer who designs an ammonia plant faces a trade-off.

→

Cold gives more ammonia, slowly

The reaction is exothermic, so cooling shifts the equilibrium right. At room temperature (25 °C) and 1 atmosphere, the mixture holds over 90% ammonia. But the N≡N triple bond (945 kJ/mol) is hard to break. At 25 °C the reaction takes years to reach equilibrium.

→

Heat and a catalyst give speed, with less ammonia

To reach equilibrium fast, engineers heat the gases to about 450 °C and pass them over an iron catalyst. The catalyst speeds up both directions equally, so it helps the system reach equilibrium sooner. It does not change the amount of ammonia at equilibrium. But at 450 °C and 1 atmosphere, the shift to the left leaves under 1% ammonia.

Engineers win some of it back. They raise the pressure to 200 atmospheres, which shifts the system right, toward fewer moles of gas. At 450 °C that lifts the yield to about 25%. They also cool the gas to liquefy the ammonia and remove it, then recycle the unreacted N2 and H2.

Common mistake

Thinking a catalyst raises the amount of ammonia at equilibrium. A catalyst speeds up both directions equally. The mixture reaches equilibrium sooner, and the equilibrium amount stays the same.

Dinitrogen tetroxide (N2O4) and nitrogen dioxide (NO2) reach dynamic equilibrium in a sealed glass syringe:

N2O4(g) [colorless] + 57.2 kJ ⇌ 2 NO2(g) [dark brown]

A student does two experiments with this mixture at 25 °C.

Experiment 1 (temperature): The student puts the sealed syringe in ice water at 0 °C. Within two minutes the dark brown gas turns almost colorless.

Experiment 2 (pressure): The student pushes the plunger in and halves the gas volume from 60 mL to 30 mL. The gas flashes darker brown for one second. Over the next 15 seconds the brown color fades until the gas is lighter than it was at the start.

(a) Use Le Chatelier’s principle and the heat in the equation to explain why the gas turns colorless in Experiment 1. [1 mark]

(b) Explain why the gas flashes darker the instant the plunger moves in Experiment 2. [1 mark]

(c) Explain why the color fades over the next 15 seconds. Use the moles of gas and Le Chatelier’s principle. [2 marks]

Scoring guide: 4 marks
  • Cooling (1 mark): You say cooling shifts the endothermic reaction left, which makes colorless N2O4.
  • Flash (1 mark): You say halving the volume doubles the particles per volume at once, before any shift.
  • Moles of gas (1 mark): You say N2O4 is 1 mole of gas and 2 NO2 is 2 moles.
  • Shift (1 mark): You say pressure shifts it toward fewer moles, so NO2 becomes N2O4 and the color fades.
Why this matters

Equilibria also set the carbon cycle of the planet. Carbon dioxide from the air dissolves in the oceans: CO2(g) ⇌ CO2(aq) ⇌ H2CO3(aq) ⇌ H+ + HCO3−. Rising CO2 in the air raises the pressure of the gas. Under Le Chatelier’s principle, more CO2 dissolves in seawater. The forward shift releases H+ ions, which acidify the ocean and threaten coral reefs.
