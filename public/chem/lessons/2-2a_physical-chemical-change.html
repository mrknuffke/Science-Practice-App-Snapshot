Lesson 1-2d · Physical vs. Chemical Change

When you heat it on the pan, does it melt or does it burn?

Drop a lump of ghee on a hot grill. It melts and slides across the metal, but it is still the same fat. Drop raw flatbread dough on the same grill. It puffs, browns and smells completely different. The first change leaves every molecule alone. The second breaks bonds and builds new substances. By the end, you can tell the two apart at the particle scale and show that atoms are conserved.

Objective
You can tell a physical change from a chemical change by tracing the bonds inside each particle. You can draw particle diagrams that keep every atom. You can explain why an open pan loses mass.
Scope
Phase changes and dissolving (physical) next to reactions (chemical). Atoms are conserved in both. Open systems and closed systems.

Core Claims

  • Physical change: The particles change state or spacing. Bonds inside each particle stay intact, so the substance keeps its identity (melting, dissolving).
  • Chemical change: Bonds break and new bonds form. Atoms regroup into new substances (reactants become products).
  • Conservation of mass: Every atom is kept in any change. The count of each atom type stays the same.

01The distinction

The distinction lies entirely in the bonds.

Every sample of matter is a collection of particles. A change can move those particles around, or it can rebuild them from the inside.

Before you label a change, trace the bonds inside each molecule. If those bonds stay untouched, the change is a physical change. The particles may speed up, spread out, slide past neighbors or mix during dissolving. Each one is still the same substance.

If bonds break and new ones form, the atoms end up in new combinations. That is a chemical change. The starting substances (reactants) disappear. New substances (products) appear with different properties.

The one idea to hold onto

A physical change moves particles apart or together and leaves each particle intact. A chemical change breaks the bonds inside particles and builds new ones.

Common mistake

Calling a change chemical because it looks dramatic or cannot be undone. Boiling makes bubbles, and torn foil stays torn, yet the particles are unchanged. Ask one question: is it the same substance afterward?

02Particle panels

How the two changes read in particle panels.

Compare a phase change with a combustion reaction. In the physical change, every molecule stays intact. In the chemical change, the atoms regroup, but the count of each atom stays the same.

Particle motion conventions

Particle diagrams show state and motion with three conventions.

  • Solid: Brackets beside the particles show vibration. The particles stay in fixed positions.
  • Liquid: Particles stay in contact with their neighbors. They slide past each other along the bottom of the container.
  • Gas: Particles are far apart. They move fast in straight lines until they collide.

Common mistake

Thinking a dissolved solid has disappeared. Sugar in water is still sucrose. The molecules are spread out between the water molecules, and stirring did not break any bond inside them.

04Where the mass goes

Where does the mass go in an open system?

The Law of Conservation of Mass says atoms are never created or destroyed. In a closed container, the mass before a change equals the mass after it.

But a cooking pan, an oven and an open beaker are open systems. Bread dough loses weight as it bakes. That does not break the law. Heat boils off water and releases carbon dioxide from the leavening agent. In an open system, those gas molecules drift into the room. The atoms still exist, but they are no longer on your scale.

To test conservation, run the change in a sealed flask (a closed system). Mix vinegar and baking soda in the flask and the gas stays trapped. The scale shows no change in mass, even though bubbles form and a new solution appears. Every atom stays inside.

Common mistake

Saying the bread lost atoms. The pan lost mass because gas escaped into the room. In a sealed flask, the same change keeps its mass.

One way to say it

Baking is a chemical change. The dough releases carbon dioxide and steam. The pan is an open system, so the gas molecules escape into the air. The mass on the scale drops. That does not break the Law of Conservation of Mass. The escaping gas carries atoms and mass into the room, and the total number of atoms stays the same.

Why This Matters

Rust on a bridge is a chemical change. Iron atoms bond with oxygen and form iron oxide, a new solid with new properties. Rust is brittle and crumbly, so it slowly weakens the steel.
