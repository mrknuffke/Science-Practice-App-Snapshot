Lesson 1-7b · Mole Conversions

Follow the map, and cancel the units.

A lab scale reads in grams. A chemical equation counts in moles. How do you convert between what you can weigh and what the atoms need? Use a conversion map and dimensional analysis. Set up the fractions so the units cancel, and the math works out. By the end, you can convert between grams and moles with any molar mass.

01Hub and spokes

The mole is always the central hub.

Follow one rule when you convert quantities in the lab: always convert through the mole. The mole links the count of particles to the mass on the scale.

This gives you the mole map. Molar mass is the bridge. It converts mass in grams to an amount in moles, or moles back to grams.

Start in grams, the unit you actually weigh. Dividing by molar mass converts to moles.
The bridge runs both ways: multiplying moles by molar mass gets you back to grams.
Moles are also the hub for counting: multiply by Avogadro's number (6.022×10²³) to reach individual particles.
The complete map. Moles sit at the hub. You never convert grams directly to particles. You always pass through moles.

This lesson focuses on the grams ↔ moles spoke. The moles ↔ particles spoke uses Avogadro's number (6.022 × 10²³) instead of molar mass. Grams never skip the mole.

Common mistake

Converting grams straight to particles. Grams and particles never connect directly. Convert grams to moles first, then moles to particles.

02Dimensional analysis

Let the units do the work.

Do not memorize when to multiply or divide. Use dimensional analysis (the factor-label method). Units behave like variables in algebra. The same unit on top and bottom cancels, and only the unit you want is left.

A conversion factor is a fraction. One mole of water (H₂O) equals 18.02 grams, so you can write two fractions that both equal 1:

1 mol H₂O18.02 g H₂O      or      18.02 g H₂O1 mol H₂O

To convert 36.04 grams of water to moles, choose the fraction placing grams in the denominator. This cancels the starting unit:

36.04 g H₂O × 1 mol H₂O18.02 g H₂O = 2.00 mol H₂O

The starting unit (g H₂O) cancels against the denominator, and you are left with mol H₂O. The units prove the setup is correct before you touch a calculator.

Common mistake

Putting the starting unit in the numerator. Grams times grams per mole gives g² per mole, which is not moles. Flip the fraction so the unit you want to cancel sits in the denominator.

04bCurricular extension, not assessed

Determining empirical and molecular formulas.

Curricular extension, not assessed

This section covers empirical and molecular formulas. Core exams do not assess it. It deepens how you use mole relationships and formulas.

An empirical formula gives the smallest whole-number mole ratio of the elements in a compound (for example CH₂O). A molecular formula gives the actual number of atoms in one molecule. Glucose is C₆H₁₂O₆, which is (CH₂O)₆.

The four-step empirical formula method

  1. Percent to mass: Assume a 100.0 g sample, so each percentage becomes grams (40.0% C is 40.0 g C).
  2. Mass to moles: Divide each element's mass by its atomic mass.
  3. Divide by the smallest: Divide every mole value by the smallest one to get the mole ratio.
  4. Multiply until whole: If a ratio is a decimal (1.5, 1.33, 1.25), multiply every number by 2, 3 or 4 to get whole numbers.

Molecular formula: Divide the true molar mass by the empirical formula mass to get n. Then multiply each empirical subscript by n.

06Explain it to yourself

Explain why treating units like variables stops you from multiplying when you should divide.

Writing it out forces your brain to organize the idea. Write your answer first, then peek and compare.

One way to say it

Units act like variables, so you can see whether they cancel. A correct setup puts the unwanted unit in the denominator, and it cancels. A wrong setup does not cancel. For example, grams times grams per mole gives g² per mole, which is not moles. Tracking the cancellation guarantees that the unit left over is the one you want, so you never multiply when you should divide.

Why This Matters

Carbon capture needs exact mole conversions. Scrubbers pull carbon dioxide out of exhaust gas. Engineers use factor-label conversions to find the mass of liquid amine that absorbs a given number of CO₂ molecules.
