How this book was built

A textbook usually arrives with no account of where it came from. This one has an account, because how it was made shapes what you should trust it for. Below: what exists so far, who wrote what, the rules it is held to, what it still cannot do, and a dated record of everything that has shipped.

01

What this is, and what it isn't

These pages are for retrieval practice and re-narration after class, not instead of it. They assume you have already met the biological ideas once with a teacher, and they are built to make you commit to an answer before showing you one.

It is not a passive reference text. It does not replace hands-on laboratory investigation. It organizes authentic phenomena (such as an endurance athlete under extreme physical exertion) and guides you through particulate bond rearrangement, feedback architecture, and data analysis.

It is also not adaptive, and it does not track who you are. Nothing you do here leaves your browser and there is no account to create.

02

Who wrote what

Built by one teacher working with AI coding agents, on the same division of labour as the General Chemistry and General Physics books.

The teacher

  • Decides what a lesson is for and what a student should be able to do at the end of it.
  • Writes and checks the biological models: every chemical reaction, molecular reactant, feedback pathway, and hormone mechanism.
  • Names authentic misconceptions (such as confusing respiration with ventilation, or assuming negative feedback means bad feedback).
  • Reads every page in a browser, in both themes, before it ships. Most defects are visual, and only a person looking at the screen catches those.
  • Signs off. Nothing goes live unreviewed.

The AI

  • Writes the code: page structure, styling, interactive simulations, and commit-reveal logic.
  • Drafts prose against a written voice contract, then gets edited.
  • Runs automated sweeps: checking design tokens, auditing glossary keys, and verifying layout invariants across mobile viewports.
  • Works under strict constraints to avoid over-complicating architectures.

The honest summary is the same across our textbooks: the AI is why this exists at all, and it also introduced its share of defects during drafting. The record at the bottom of this page tracks the historical evolution plainly.

03

The rules it is held to

Shared with the chemistry and physics texts, because they were learned through hard experience:

  • Verify all biological facts. Never trust memory. Chemical equations for respiration, hormone sources, target effectors, and set-point data are checked against authoritative curriculum sources.
  • Commit before reveal. No question card shows its answer to a student who has not answered. Free-text inputs require substantive reasoning before unlocking scoring rubrics.
  • Wrong-answer feedback teaches or it does not ship. "Incorrect, try again" is banned. Feedback has to explain why that particular distractor was tempting.
  • Honest beats pretty. If a diagram cannot be made biologically honest at the level shown, the claim gets simplified rather than faked.
  • Explain the concept before naming it. Terms are introduced through physical scenarios and plain language before introducing technical jargon. Defined terms carry a dotted underline and a tooltip definition.
  • Render before declaring it done. Open it in a browser, in both themes, at both desktop and phone widths.
  • No build step. Zero-build static HTML, CSS, and classic JavaScript that will open in any browser a decade from now without compilation.
04

What's underneath

A lean, zero-build stack adapted from SAS General Physics. Plain HTML, CSS, and vanilla JavaScript loaded directly by the browser without bundlers or heavy libraries.

Every lesson loads one shared set of files that all of our textbooks use: base.css and lesson.css for type, layout, and components, and core.js, widgets.js, and sim.js for the top bar, the question widgets, and the simulations. This book adds tokens.css for its emerald palette and biological colors, components.css for layouts only this book uses, book.js for its settings and the dashboard, and glossary.js for hover and tap definitions of core biological vocabulary.

Every page is verified by automated preflight checks before any deploy to ensure zero broken links, no script crashes, and no horizontal overflow on mobile viewports.

05

What it still gets wrong

Listed openly because an uncritical colophon is not trustworthy.

  • It covers Unit 1 and the start of Unit 2. The rest of Unit 2 (Ecology and the Carbon Cycle) is being written now. Genetics and Microevolution come later.
  • No automated spaced repetition. While Chemistry includes an SRS scheduling engine, Biology currently relies on within-lesson active recall and summative reviews.
  • Early in classroom testing. The lessons mirror authentic student practice sets from Singapore American School, but live classroom pacing feedback will refine explanations over time.

22 October 2026

Fixes after a full review

  • Nothing to read before you predict. Until you commit your prediction, every reading in a simulation shows a dash, and Record does nothing.
  • Play to go on. When a simulation stops on a data point, it tells you, and the Pause button reads Play. Start over always runs the model again.
  • Clearer science. The respiration check now says that forming new bonds releases more energy than breaking the old ones takes. In lesson 1-3 the blood returns to normal once the athlete drinks. Type 2 diabetes in lesson 1-4 has its own curve, still above 200 mg/dL after two hours.
  • Feedback starts with the reason. No answer opens with "Right." The review's quick checks now say why each wrong answer was tempting.
  • Tap a bold word on a phone to see its meaning. Printing from dark mode now gives light pictures of each simulation, and lesson 1-1 prints with its atoms in view.
14 October 2026

Four rebuilt simulations

  • Four new simulations. Each lesson has a rebuilt simulation that starts with a question and a prediction. The model stays locked until you commit your prediction. You change one or two controls, press Record to keep a row in a table, and answer a question that uses only your rows.
  • Respiration follows the atoms. In lesson 1-1 you watch one glucose and six oxygen molecules break apart. The same atoms leave as carbon dioxide and water. Two bars show where the energy goes.
  • Thermoregulation, osmoregulation, and blood glucose run as loops. Each model reacts over time, and the numbers in each model match the data table in its lesson.
  • Pause and step. Every simulation has a Pause button, and stops when it is off screen. With reduced motion turned on, it starts paused and gives you a Step button.
13 October 2026

One shared design for all of our textbooks

  • Same look as chemistry and physics. Lesson text, headings, cards, buttons, and tables now come from one shared set of files. Each lesson opens with a title, three short rows (alignment, objective, scope), and a Start button.
  • Checkpoints mark your answer. Every checkpoint shows "Your answer" on the option you chose and a check mark on the right answer, and the feedback for your choice comes first.
  • Answers wait for an attempt. The scoring guide stays locked until you write an answer, and "Show answers" on recall blanks stays locked until you press Check.
  • One theme switch. The light and dark choice is saved once and shared with the other textbooks and the main app.
  • Lesson navigation. Every lesson ends with links to the previous and next lessons, and the contents menu lists each section.
  • Plainer words. Every lesson, checkpoint, and scoring guide is rewritten in shorter sentences. Terms that go past the standards (for example the parts of a kidney tube, or how a transporter works inside a cell) are cut, or moved into optional Enrichment blocks with a dashed outline.
  • Every answer explains itself. Each answer option has its own feedback that says why it was tempting, and no feedback starts with "Correct!".
  • A common mistake in every concept section. A short callout names the wrong idea students most often hold.
  • Quick checks in the review. The Unit 1 review now opens with seven marked questions and a sorting task built from the lesson checkpoints.
