01Orientation · Hook
Phenomenon

Alpine cold and desert heat. Leo hikes an alpine ridge. The air is 38°C in the arid foothills and -5°C near the snowline, with a 30 km/h wind. Despite these swings, his vital organs stay between 36.5°C and 37.5°C.

The ability to keep a steady internal state while conditions outside change is called homeostasis. The body holds each variable near a set point. A small drift triggers a response that pushes the variable back toward its target.

02Concept · Feedback loop

The four parts of a feedback loop

Every control system of this kind has the same four parts. Together they form a negative feedback loop:

  • Stimulus: A change moves a variable away from its set point. Freezing air, for example, lowers skin and blood temperature.
  • Receptors (sensors): Temperature sensors in the skin and in the blood vessels detect the shift. They send nerve signals to the brain.
  • Control center (hypothalamus): This part of the brain works as the body's thermostat. It compares the signals with the 37.0°C set point.
  • Effectors: Organs and tissues carry out the response that opposes the change. Examples are blood vessels in the skin, skeletal muscles, and sweat glands.
The one idea to hold onto

"Negative" means the response goes against the change. If temperature drops, the loop saves heat and makes more. If temperature rises, the loop releases heat faster.

Common mistake

Many students hear "negative" and think the feedback is harmful. It is not. "Negative" only says that the response acts against the change.

03Physiology · Effector responses

How the body responds to cold and to heat

To hold core temperature steady, the hypothalamus coordinates two sets of responses:

Cold challenge responses

  • Vasoconstriction: Small muscles around the blood vessels in the skin tighten and narrow the vessels. Blood moves toward the body core, so less heat escapes.
  • Shivering: Skeletal muscles contract in fast, involuntary bursts. They use ATP, and the shivering muscles make heat.
  • Goosebumps: Tiny muscles pull the hairs upright. The hairs trap a layer of still air against the skin.

Heat challenge responses

  • Vasodilation: The blood vessels in the skin widen. More warm blood flows near the surface and gives off heat to the air.
  • Sweating: Nerves signal the sweat glands to release sweat onto the skin. As sweat evaporates, it takes heat from the skin.
  • Behavior: A person slows down and relaxes. The hairs lie flat.
Common mistake

Cold fingers do not mean the body has failed. They show the response working. Narrow skin vessels send blood to the core and leave less for the hands.

06Synthesis · Scientific argumentation

Evidence, claim, reasoning

Use the feedback loop to explain a real emergency.

Leo falls into an icy stream

Written response4 marks

Leo slips and falls into an icy glacial stream (4°C water). Within 60 seconds of crawling onto the snowbank, he shivers violently, his lips look pale and blue, and his breathing speeds up. His core temperature reads 36.4°C.

Your task: Write one paragraph that names the stimulus, the sensors, the control center, and two effectors. Explain how they keep his core temperature from collapsing.

Scoring guide
  • Claim (1 mark): You say the cold plunge starts a feedback loop that the hypothalamus directs.
  • You say the loop raises heat production and cuts heat loss.
  • Evidence (1 mark): You cite violent shivering, pale blue skin, and faster breathing.
  • You link the faster breathing to the oxygen that working muscles need.
  • Sensor and control (1 mark): You say skin sensors tell the hypothalamus that temperature fell below 37.0°C.
  • Effectors (1 mark): You say the hypothalamus narrows the skin vessels, which cuts blood flow to the cold skin.
  • You say it also starts shivering, which uses ATP and makes heat.
