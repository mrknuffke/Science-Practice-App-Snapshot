Part 1: Main ideas digest

Pillar 1: Designing a solution (HS-LS2-7)

A good design starts from the problem. Its criteria say what it must do. Its constraints are the limits it must work within, such as cost, space and public support.

Version 1.0 is a first idea. You then test it against evidence, find its weak points and change it. Each change should be backed by data, not by a hunch.

Pillar 3: Energy flows, matter cycles (HS-LS2-4)

Each step up a food chain passes on only a small share of the energy. In Silver Springs the steps passed on 16%, 11% and 5.5%, which is why top predators are rare. Energy leaves each level as heat, so the sun must keep supplying it.

Matter is different: atoms are reused. In a sealed system like Biosphere 2, carbon had nowhere to go. Microbes turned soil carbon into carbon dioxide, and the concrete locked much of it away.

To estimate a carrying capacity, multiply density by area. Density changes with scale: a rich forest holds far more animals per km² than an island's average.

Reindeer on St. Matthew Island

Problem 1 · Carrying capacity4 marks

St. Matthew Island is a remote island in the Bering Sea, off Alaska. It had no reindeer and no large predators. In 1944, 29 reindeer were released there as an emergency food supply.

Reindeer on St. Matthew Island
YearReindeer
194429
Summer 19636,000
Summer 196642

Source: Klein, 1968, Journal of Wildlife Management 32: 350 to 367. Most of the reindeer died in the winter of 1963 to 1964. By then they had overgrazed their winter food, lichen.

(a) Give two reasons the herd could grow so large.
(b) Calculate the percentage of the 1963 herd that was gone by 1966.
(c) Use the idea of carrying capacity to explain the crash.

Scoring guide
  • (a) Growth (1 mark): You give two of these: no predators, plenty of untouched lichen, no other large grazers.
  • (b) Calculation (1 mark): 6,000 minus 42 is 5,958, and 5,958 divided by 6,000 is about 99%.
  • (c) Overshoot (1 mark): You explain that the herd grew far past the number the island's food could support.
  • (c) Crash (1 mark): You explain that the reindeer ate the lichen faster than it regrew. The food ran out, and most starved.
  • The carrying capacity fell as the lichen was destroyed.

Wolves and moose on Isle Royale

Problem 2 · Energy and populations4 marks

Isle Royale is an island in Lake Superior. Moose eat its plants, and wolves eat the moose. Scientists count both every winter.

Moose and wolves on Isle Royale
CountNumber
Moose, winter 2019just over 2,000
Moose, winter 2024840
Wolves, winter 202430

Source: Isle Royale Winter Study, Michigan Technological University, 2024.

(a) In 2024, how many moose were there for each wolf?
(b) Use energy flow to explain why there are far fewer wolves than moose.
(c) Calculate the percentage fall in moose from 2019 to 2024.
(d) Name one limiting factor for the moose, and explain how it works.

Scoring guide
  • (a) Ratio (1 mark): 840 divided by 30 is 28 moose for each wolf.
  • (b) Energy (1 mark): You explain that only a small share of the moose's energy passes up to wolves.
  • Moose use most of it to live, and it leaves as heat. So the wolf level supports far fewer animals.
  • (c) Calculation (1 mark): 2,000 minus 840 is 1,160, and 1,160 divided by 2,000 is 58%, or nearly 60%.
  • (d) Limiting factor (1 mark): You name one, such as wolves killing moose or a shortage of plants to eat.
  • You explain how it lowers moose survival or births.
