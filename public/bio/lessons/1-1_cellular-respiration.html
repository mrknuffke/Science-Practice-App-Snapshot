Lesson 1-1 · Cellular Respiration & Energy Transfer

The sugar you burn leaves your body as breath and water.

Maya swims harder, and she breathes faster. Her muscle cells use more glucose and more oxygen. The carbon from that glucose leaves as carbon dioxide, and the hydrogen leaves as water. This page follows the atoms and the energy.

Alignment

Objective
You can explain that cellular respiration breaks the bonds in glucose and oxygen and forms new bonds. You can show that every atom is accounted for and that energy moves to ATP.
Scope
The bonds that break, the bonds that form, where each atom goes, and where the energy goes.
01Orientation · Hook
Phenomenon

Maya's muscles at full effort. Maya is an endurance swimmer. She is training for a long open-water channel crossing. She swims against strong currents. Her muscle cells need a steady supply of usable energy to power each stroke and to keep her core warm in cold water.

When Maya moves from a gentle warm-up to a full sprint, her breathing deepens, her heart rate climbs, and her body temperature rises. Which chemical changes inside her muscle cells supply this extra energy?

To see why Maya breathes faster and uses more fuel, look inside her muscle cells. Cells do not burn food like a campfire. They run a series of chemical reactions called cellular respiration.

02Foundations · Chemical reaction

Bonds break and new bonds form

In aerobic cellular respiration, cells take in two inputs. One is glucose, a sugar from food. The other is oxygen, which the lungs absorb. Inside the mitochondria, the cell breaks the bonds that hold these molecules together.

Glucose + Oxygen → Carbon Dioxide + Water + ATP (usable energy)
C6H12O6 + 6 O2 → 6 CO2 + 6 H2O + ATP

No atom is destroyed. Every carbon, hydrogen, and oxygen atom in the inputs appears in the outputs:

  • 6 carbon atoms: They start in glucose. They leave in 6 molecules of carbon dioxide (CO2).
  • 12 hydrogen atoms: They start in glucose. They leave in 6 molecules of water (H2O).
  • 18 oxygen atoms: 6 come from glucose and 12 come from 6 molecules of oxygen (O2). They leave as 12 atoms in CO2 and 6 atoms in H2O.
The one idea to hold onto

Matter is rearranged, and energy is transferred. Cells do not turn matter into energy. When the cell breaks the bonds in glucose and oxygen, the atoms settle into more stable arrangements in carbon dioxide and water. The cell captures part of the energy this releases in ATP, its usable energy carrier.

Common mistake

Many students say that cells make energy. Cells never make energy. They move it. The energy in ATP was already stored in the bonds of glucose.

05Evidence · Data analysis

A lab test with spinach leaf discs

To measure the rate of cellular respiration, students test spinach leaf discs in different sugar solutions. They remove the air from the discs first.

Laboratory protocol

Experiment: Students place sunk leaf discs in three beakers in the dark. One has plain water, one has 0.2M sucrose, and one has 0.2M fructose. As the cells respire, gas builds up inside the leaf, and the discs float to the surface.

Experimental analysis task3 marks

Variables and trend: Name the independent variable and the dependent variable in this study. Then say which solution gave the fastest rate of cellular respiration.

Scoring guide
  • Independent variable (1 mark): You name the type of solution: water, 0.2M sucrose, or 0.2M fructose.
  • Dependent variable (1 mark): You name the mean time for 10 discs to float, in seconds.
  • Fastest condition (1 mark): You say sucrose is fastest, with 180 s against 270 s and 420 s.
  • You say faster floating means faster respiration, because the discs fill with gas sooner.
