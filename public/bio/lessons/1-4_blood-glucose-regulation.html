01Orientation · Hook
Phenomenon

Meal digestion and fasting. Sonia eats a high-carbohydrate lunch at 12:00 PM. Over the next hour, digested glucose surges into her blood. By 3:30 PM she is fasting during light afternoon exercise, and her blood glucose dips toward its lower limit. Through this whole window, her brain gets a continuous supply of glucose fuel.

The body holds blood glucose between 70 and 110 mg/dL. This control is called blood glucose regulation. Glucose is the key fuel for the brain and for working muscle. After a meal, the body locks away the extra sugar in storage. When glucose falls, it releases the stored sugar to restore the level.

02Endocrine regulation · Hormonal control

Two hormones that work against each other

The pancreas works as both sensor and control center for glucose balance. Two kinds of pancreas cells release hormones with opposite effects:

The insulin pathway (high glucose)

  • Stimulus: Blood glucose rises above the 100 mg/dL baseline after a meal.
  • Sensor and control: Beta (β) cells in the pancreas detect the high glucose and release insulin into the blood.
  • Target tissues and effector action: Insulin tells liver cells and muscle cells to take in glucose. The cells store the glucose as glycogen.
  • Response: Blood glucose returns to normal, and insulin release stops.

The glucagon pathway (low glucose)

  • Stimulus: Blood glucose drops below 80 mg/dL during exercise or between meals.
  • Sensor and control: Alpha (α) cells in the pancreas sense the falling glucose and release glucagon into the blood.
  • Target tissues and effector action: Glucagon acts mainly on the liver. Liver cells break glycogen back into glucose, and they can also make new glucose.
  • Response: Glucose enters the blood, and the level returns to baseline. Glucagon release then stops.
Common mistake

Many students think insulin breaks down glycogen. It does the opposite. Insulin stores glucose, and glucagon releases it.

03Physiology · Diabetic disruptions

When the loop breaks: diabetes

Glucose cannot cross the cell membrane on its own. Insulin tells muscle and liver cells to take glucose in. When the insulin signal fails, glucose stays in the blood.

Type 1 diabetes

The immune system attacks and destroys the beta cells in the pancreas. The body makes almost no insulin. Blood glucose can climb above 250 mg/dL, yet the cells starve because they cannot take the glucose in.

Type 2 diabetes

The pancreas makes plenty of insulin, but cells stop responding well to it. Blood glucose stays high, and the body keeps making extra insulin.

Enrichment

How insulin lets glucose into a cell

Extra depth. The rest of the lesson works without it.

A cell has doors for glucose, called glucose transporters. In muscle and fat cells, most of these transporters wait inside the cell. When insulin arrives, the transporters move to the cell surface, and glucose flows in. Liver cells keep their transporters at the surface all the time. In the liver, insulin's job is to make the cells store the glucose as glycogen.

In Type 1 diabetes there is no insulin signal, so the transporters stay inside. In Type 2 diabetes the signal is weak, so fewer transporters reach the surface.

Common mistake

Many students think high blood sugar means the cells are well fed. In diabetes the cells can starve while the blood is full of glucose.

06Synthesis · Scientific argumentation

Evidence, claim, reasoning

Use the data in section 04 to compare Patient A and Patient C.

Compare two patients after a glucose drink

Written response4 marks

After a 75 g glucose drink, Patient A peaks at 138 mg/dL at 30 minutes and returns to 92 mg/dL by 120 minutes. Patient C starts at 165 mg/dL, climbs to 275 mg/dL at 60 minutes, and is still at 260 mg/dL after 120 minutes.

Prompt: Explain which patient shows a working negative feedback loop. Contrast how each patient's body moves glucose into cells.

Scoring guide
  • Claim (1 mark): You say Patient A's loop works and returns glucose to baseline.
  • You say Patient C's loop has failed, which is diabetes.
  • Evidence (1 mark): You cite Patient A returning to 92 mg/dL by 120 minutes.
  • You cite Patient C at 260 mg/dL, above the 200 mg/dL diagnosis line.
  • Reasoning, Patient A (1 mark): You say high glucose makes beta cells release insulin.
  • You say insulin makes liver and muscle cells take in glucose and store it.
  • Reasoning, Patient C (1 mark): You say the loop is cut at one point.
  • You say there is no insulin (Type 1), or the cells do not respond (Type 2).
  • You say glucose then stays in the blood.
