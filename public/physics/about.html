How this book was built.

A textbook usually arrives with no account of where it came from. This one has an account, because how it was made shapes what you should trust it for. Below: what exists so far, who wrote what, the rules it is held to, what it still cannot do, and a dated record of everything that has shipped.

01

What this is, and what it isn't

These pages are for retrieval practice and re-narration after class, not instead of it. They assume you have already met the ideas once, with a teacher, and they are built to make you commit to an answer before they show you one.

It is not a reference text. It does not cover the whole of physics, or even the whole of mechanics. As of today it covers Level 1 only. If you are looking for material on forces, energy, fields or waves, it is not here yet, and this page will say so plainly until it is.

It is also not adaptive, and it does not know who you are. Nothing you do here leaves your browser and there is no account to create.

02

Who wrote what

Built by one teacher working with AI coding agents, on the same division of labour as the General Chemistry book. The specific tools have changed several times already and will change again, so naming them is not useful. The split is the part that matters.

The teacher

  • Decides what a lesson is for and what a student should be able to do at the end of it.
  • Writes and checks the physics: every value, every sign convention, every worked solution in the review.
  • Names the misconceptions. The line that predicts your wrong move before you make it comes from watching students make exactly that move.
  • Reads every page in a browser, in both themes, before it ships. Most defects here are visual, and only a person looking at the screen catches those.
  • Signs off. Nothing goes live unreviewed.

The AI

  • Writes the code: page structure, styling, the graphing and simulation canvases, the Reference Center drawer, the progress logic.
  • Drafts prose against a written voice contract, then gets edited.
  • Does the tedious sweeps that people do badly: retrofitting a token across a thousand lines, auditing every glossary link, finding the canvas that quietly stopped drawing.
  • Works under constraints written to catch the specific ways these models fail: inventing plausible-looking physics, over-building a simple thing, silently "improving" code nobody asked it to touch.

The honest summary is the same as the chemistry book's: the AI is why this exists at all, and it also introduced a fair share of the defects listed below. Both are true, and the history at the bottom of this page is deliberately unflattering about it.

03

The rules it is held to

Shared with the chemistry book, because they were learned the same way: each one was broken at least once and the result was worse than the rule.

  • Verify all physics. Never trust memory. Any number that reaches a student page is checked, not recalled. A confident, wrong, nicely formatted physics claim is the worst thing this project could produce.
  • Commit before reveal. No widget shows its answer to a student who has not answered. Every simulation asks for a prediction first, and it stays locked until you commit one.
  • Wrong-answer feedback teaches or it does not ship. "Incorrect, review the section above" is banned. Feedback has to name why that particular wrong answer was tempting.
  • Honest beats pretty. If a diagram cannot be made correct at the level shown, the claim gets simplified rather than fudged.
  • Explain the concept before naming it. Terms are defined in plain English first, then labelled. Defined terms carry a dotted underline and a hover definition.
  • Render before declaring it done. Open it in a browser, in both themes, at both widths, and look at it. A surprising number of defects are found only by somebody finally doing this.
  • No build step. Static HTML, CSS and JavaScript, no framework and no bundler, so it will still open in a browser in ten years.
04

What's underneath

A deliberately small stack. The chemistry book's runtime is around 4,500 lines; this one is around 350. That is a choice, not an unfinished state: this book needs graphing canvases and a reference drawer, and it does not need chemistry's widget framework, so it does not carry it.

Four files do the work. tokens.css holds the cobalt and electric-blue theme, the type scale and the diagram geometry. components.css holds the layout primitives, figure tiers and mathematical typography. core.js runs the dynamic table of contents, the reveal observer and the commit-before-reveal gates. reference-center.js is the slide-out drawer: Level 1 equations, physical constants, a six-pattern graphing and linearization matrix, and a scientific calculator, available from every page.

Since 20 September 2026 every page here is checked automatically before any deploy: each lesson is requested along with every script it loads, and each is opened in a real browser to confirm it raises no errors and renders no stray markup. Before that date nothing checked this book at all, and it could have broken without anyone finding out.

05

What it still gets wrong

Listed because a colophon that only says flattering things is not worth reading.

  • It covers Levels 1 and 2 so far. Eight lessons and two reviews. The dashboard describes a course covering mechanics, energy, fields and waves; Levels 3 through 5 are in active development.
  • No spaced repetition. The chemistry book schedules your review for you. This one does not. You get retrieval practice inside a lesson, and then it is on you to come back.
  • No print or export path for lessons. The review prints properly, on purpose. The interactive lessons do not.
  • The dashboard's lesson list is hand-maintained. Resolved 27 September 2026. The dashboard now renders directly from the curriculum manifest (lessons.json) at runtime, so lessons populate and update automatically without markup duplication.
  • Nobody has taught a full cycle with it yet. The chemistry book has been through real classes and carries the scars. This one has been through review cycles and a request for teacher feedback. Expect the pacing to be wrong in places.

One name for the book

  • SAS General Physics. The book now uses one name everywhere: the dashboard heading, every lesson's tab title and this page, matching SAS General Chemistry and SAS General Biology. Level and mission names are unchanged.
23 October 2026

All thirteen simulations rebuilt

  • A question, a prediction, then the sim. Each simulation now opens with the question it answers. You commit a prediction before the controls and the readings unlock.
  • Try this and Record. Each simulation has numbered steps that say what to change and what to look for. A Record button saves a row of readings, and a question after the sim asks you to compare two recorded rows.
  • Pause and reduced motion. Every moving simulation has a Pause button. If your device asks for less motion, it starts paused and offers a Step button.
  • Models fixed. Friction now slows a moving cart that is pushed less than it is held back. A sliding block slows down when you lower the ramp. The force collider shows a force only while the bumpers touch, and it keeps the momentum it started with. One value of g (9.8 N/kg) is used in every sim and every checkpoint.
  • Changed. The Level 1 Boss Challenge is now Projectile Precision, and its answer is printed after each launch. The acceleration-angle graph in the ramp sim is part of the Enrichment view. The rocket burn-off mode in the acceleration rig is gone.
22 October 2026

Plainer words, new checks and optional depth

  • Every lesson opens with a question. Each lesson has a new opening title and a short plain statement of what you will be able to do.

  • Optional depth is marked. Material beyond the core, such as two-dimensional launches, forces at angles and the trigonometry of ramps, sits in dashed boxes tagged Enrichment. The core lesson works without it.
