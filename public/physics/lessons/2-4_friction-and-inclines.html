02 Friction

Static vs. Kinetic Friction

Even smoothly polished surfaces look like jagged mountain ranges under a microscope.

Microscopic peaks called asperities touch and form electrostatic cold-weld bonds across contact points.

Static Friction (fs)

Acts when two surfaces are at rest relative to each other:

0 ≤ fs ≤ μs FN

Static friction matches applied force up to a maximum threshold. It prevents sliding until broken.

Kinetic Friction (fk)

Acts once relative sliding motion has begun:

fk = μk FN

Kinetic friction is constant at ordinary speeds. It continuously opposes the sliding direction.

Why Static Friction Exceeds Kinetic Friction

For nearly all material pairs, the coefficient of friction for static friction is larger than the one for kinetic friction:

μs > μk

When objects rest together, contact asperities settle deeply into valleys. They form strong intermolecular bonds.

During sliding motion, asperities skip rapidly across peaks without time to settle into deep bonds.

Common mistake

Static friction is not a fixed number. It matches the push, up to a maximum. A box that does not move can have a small friction force or a large one, depending on how hard you push.

03Forces on a ramp

A Block on a Ramp

A block sits on a ramp. Three forces act on it: its weight, the normal force from the ramp, and friction from the ramp.

The weight points straight down. The normal force points straight out of the ramp surface. Friction points along the ramp, against the motion or against the push to move.

Tilt the ramp, and the weight does two jobs. One part pulls the block down along the ramp. We call it Fg∥. The other part presses the block into the ramp.

A steeper ramp makes Fg∥ bigger. It also presses the block less into the ramp, so the normal force gets smaller.

The block stays at rest while static friction can match Fg∥. It slips when Fg∥ becomes bigger than the maximum static friction.

The mass of the block does not change the angle where it slips. A heavier block has a bigger Fg∥ and a bigger maximum friction. Both grow by the same factor. The slip angle depends on the two surfaces.

Common mistake

A heavier block does not slip at a smaller angle. The pull along the ramp and the maximum friction both grow with the mass, so the slip angle stays the same.

05 Direction of friction

Direction of Friction Along an Incline

One rule governs the direction of friction: friction always opposes the actual or impending relative motion.

Consider pushing a heavy crate on an incline:

Pushing Crate Uphill

Motion is directed uphill (+x). Both downhill gravity and friction fight your push:

ΣFx = Fpush − Fg∥ − fk

You must supply enough force to overcome both the pull of gravity along the ramp and kinetic friction.

Lowering Crate Downhill

Motion is directed downhill (+x). Kinetic friction points uphill to help you:

ΣFx = Fg∥ − Fhold − fk

Friction assists your restraining hold, reducing the force needed to lower the crate safely.

Common mistake

Friction does not always point uphill, and it does not always point against your push. It points against the motion of the surface, or against the push that tries to start the motion.

Tilted Reference Frames and the Critical Angle

Enrichment

Tilted Reference Frames

Extra depth. The rest of the lesson works without it.

On an inclined ramp, standard horizontal and vertical axes complicate calculations because acceleration is slanted.

Instead, we rotate our coordinate frame to align with the physical constraints of the ramp surface:

  • Parallel Axis (x): Along the ramp surface (downhill defined as positive).
  • Perpendicular Axis (y): Perpendicular to the ramp surface (away from ramp defined as positive).

Resolving the Gravity Vector

True gravity (Fg = mg) points straight down toward Earth's center. We decompose it into components:

Fg⊥ = mg cosθ          Fg∥ = mg sinθ

Because the block does not sink into or fly off the ramp, vertical equilibrium holds along the perpendicular axis:

ΣFy = FN − Fg⊥ = 0   &implies;   FN = mg cosθ

As the ramp angle θ increases, cosθ decreases. Therefore, normal force decreases as the ramp steepens.

The Critical Slip Angle

At what angle does a resting block begin to slide? Slipping occurs when downhill gravity exceeds maximum static friction:

mg sinθc = μs mg cosθc   &implies;   tanθc = μs

Notice that mass cancels completely. The critical slip angle depends only on the materials in contact.

A 20,000-kilogram runaway truck enters an upward escape ramp at 28 meters per second. The ramp is tilted at an angle of 15° above horizontal. Loose pea gravel provides a kinetic friction coefficient of 0.40.

A highway inspector asks whether an available 100-meter ramp is long enough to stop the truck safely.

Evaluate this scenario using the Evidence → Claim → Reasoning (ECR) framework.

Scoring guide
  • Givens (1 mark): You list vi = 28 m/s, θ = 15°, μk = 0.40 and a ramp length of 100 m.
  • Deceleration (1 mark): You resolve the forces along the ramp to find a = −6.32 m/s².
  • Stopping distance (1 mark): You use kinematics to find d = 62.0 m.
  • You say 62.0 m is within the 100 m ramp limit.
Model response (Evidence, Claim, Reasoning)
  • Evidence: Truck mass is 20,000 kg. Initial speed is 28 m/s, final speed is 0 m/s. Ramp angle is 15°, μk is 0.40, and ramp length is 100 m.
  • Claim: Yes, the ramp is long enough. The truck stops in 62.0 meters, well within the 100-meter safety margin.
  • Reasoning: Downhill deceleration: a = −g(sin 15° + μk cos 15°) = −9.8(0.2588 + 0.40 × 0.9659) = −6.32 m/s². By kinematics: d = vi² / (2|a|) = (28)² / (2 × 6.32) = 784 / 12.64 = 62.0 m. Because 62.0 m < 100 m, the truck stops safely.
