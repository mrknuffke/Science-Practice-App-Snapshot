At liftoff, the Saturn V rocket accelerated upward at roughly 12 meters per second squared.

Two minutes later, right before first-stage burnout, its acceleration surged to nearly 40 meters per second squared.

The rocket engines did not produce more thrust. The engines delivered nearly constant thrust throughout the ascent.

Why did the acceleration triple? The rocket burned thousands of kilograms of kerosene and liquid oxygen every second.

As vehicle mass plummeted, the same thrust caused much larger acceleration. This connection is Newton's Second Law of Motion.

02 The second law

Proportional Reasoning & The Second Law

When the vector sum of all external forces is nonzero, the object accelerates. Newton wrote this relationship as:

ΣF = ma   ⇔   a = ΣF / m

This equation gives two proportionalities:

Direct: Acceleration & Net Force

Acceleration is directly proportional to net force for constant mass:

a ∝ ΣF   (constant m)

Doubling the net force on an object doubles its acceleration. Tripling net force triples acceleration.

Inverse: Acceleration & Mass

Acceleration is inversely proportional to mass for constant net force:

a ∝ 1 / m   (constant ΣF)

Doubling object mass cuts acceleration in half. Tripling mass reduces acceleration to one third.

Direction of Acceleration

Mass is a positive scalar. Therefore, the acceleration vector always points in the exact direction of the net force vector:

dir(a) = dir(ΣF)
Common mistake

An object does not always move in the direction of the net force. A car braking while heading north moves north, but its acceleration and net force point south.

Forces in Two Dimensions

Enrichment

Component Equations in Two Dimensions

Extra depth. The rest of the lesson works without it.

In two-dimensional problems, forces act along independent perpendicular axes. We resolve Newton's Second Law into separate component equations:

ΣFx = max          ΣFy = may

Forces along the x-axis affect only horizontal acceleration. Forces along the y-axis affect only vertical acceleration.

Worked Example: Horizontal Sled with Resistance

A 20-kilogram sled is pulled across icy snow with a horizontal forward force of 70 Newtons. Kinetic friction resists with 30 Newtons.

Vertical Axis (y):
ΣFy = FN − Fg = 0.
FN = Fg = mg.
FN = 20 × 9.8 = 196 N.
ay = 0 m/s² (Equilibrium).
Horizontal Axis (x):
ΣFx = Fapp − fk.
ΣFx = 70 N − 30 N = 40 N.
ax = ΣFx / m.
ax = 40 N / 20 kg = 2.0 m/s².

The vertical forces balance completely. The unbalanced horizontal force causes steady forward acceleration of 2.0 meters per second squared.

05 Apparent weight

Apparent Weight & Elevator Physics

When you stand on a bathroom scale, what does it measure? The scale does not directly read gravitational force.

The scale measures the upward normal force (FN) it exerts against your feet to support you.

We call this supporting contact force your apparent weight. Let upward be positive. Newton's Second Law gives:

ΣFy = FN − mg = may   &implies;   FN = m(g + ay)

Upward Acceleration

ay > 0 (speeding up up, or slowing down down)
FN = m(g + a) > mg

The scale pushes with extra force to accelerate you upward. You feel heavier than normal.

Downward Acceleration

ay < 0 (speeding up down, or slowing down up)
FN = m(g − |a|) < mg

The floor drops away slightly. The scale needs less force to support you. You feel lighter.

Free Fall Weightlessness

ay = −g (cable snapped or orbital orbit)
FN = m(g − g) = 0

Scale reads zero. True weight still acts, but without support force, apparent weight vanishes.

Common mistake

A downward acceleration does not always mean weightlessness. The scale reads zero only when the acceleration is exactly −g, in free fall.

Problem 2.2: Mars Rover Emergency Braking

Written response3 marks

An 800-kilogram robotic rover travels across a flat Martian plain at 15 meters per second. The terrain scanner detects a steep crevasse 30 meters ahead. The emergency magnetic friction brakes can produce a maximum retarding force of 3500 Newtons.

Will the rover stop safely before reaching the edge of the crevasse?

Evaluate this scenario using the Evidence → Claim → Reasoning (ECR) framework.

Scoring guide
  • Givens (1 mark): You list m = 800 kg, vi = 15 m/s, vf = 0, dlimit = 30 m and Fmax = 3500 N.
  • Calculation (1 mark): You find the needed acceleration, a = −3.75 m/s².
  • You use ΣF = ma to find the needed force (3000 N) or the stopping distance (25.7 m).
  • Conclusion (1 mark): You say the rover stops safely.
  • You compare 3000 N with 3500 N, or 25.7 m with 30 m.
Model response (Evidence, Claim, Reasoning)
  • Evidence: Rover mass is 800 kg. Initial velocity is 15 m/s, final velocity is 0 m/s, and available stopping distance is 30 m. Maximum available braking force is 3500 N.
  • Claim: Yes, the rover will stop safely. The required stopping force of 3000 N is within the 3500 N braking capacity. The rover stops in 25.7 meters.
  • Reasoning: To stop in 30 m: a = −vi² / (2d) = −(15)² / 60 = −3.75 m/s². By Newton's Second Law: ΣF = ma = 800 kg × 3.75 m/s² = 3000 N. Because 3000 N ≤ 3500 N, the brakes supply sufficient force. At maximum braking (3500 N), actual stopping distance is only 25.7 m.
