02 Force and inertia

What Is a Force?

In physics, a force is an interaction between two objects consisting of a push or a pull. Forces are vectors with magnitude and direction.

The SI unit of force is the newton (N). One newton is the force that accelerates a 1-kilogram mass at 1 meter per second squared:

1 N = 1 kg · m/s²

Contact Forces

Forces produced when two macroscopic surfaces or objects physically touch:

  • Normal Force (FN): Perpendicular support push from a surface.
  • Tension (FT): Pull transmitted through a taut string or cable.
  • Friction (f): Tangential drag opposing sliding motion.
  • Applied Force (Fapp): Direct physical push by a motor or person.

Field Forces (Action-at-a-Distance)

Forces exerted across space without physical contact via invisible fields:

  • Gravity (Fg): Downward attraction toward a planetary mass.
  • Electrostatic (Fe): Attraction or repulsion between electric charges.
  • Magnetic (FB): Force between magnetic poles and moving charges.

Inertia and Mass

Inertia is the natural tendency of an object to resist changes in its velocity. An object at rest resists moving. An object in motion resists stopping.

Mass (m) is the quantitative measure of inertia. The more mass an object has, the harder it is to change its speed or direction.

Common mistake

Mass versus weight: do not mix them up.

Mass is an intrinsic scalar property measured in kilograms (kg). It never changes with location.

Weight is the downward gravitational pull on that mass: Fg = m · g (measured in newtons). On the Moon, your mass stays the same, but your weight drops by 83%.

03 Newton's first law

The Law of Inertia

Newton's First Law describes what an object does when the forces on it balance:

Newton's First Law

An object at rest remains at rest. An object in motion continues at constant velocity, unless acted upon by a non-zero net external force.

The key term is net force (ΣF). Net force is the vector sum of all external forces on the object at the same moment.

ΣF = F1 + F2 + ... + Fn

Balanced Forces and Equilibrium

When the forces are balanced, the vector sum of all external forces equals zero. The object is in equilibrium. Because the net force is zero, the acceleration is zero:

ΣFx = 0   and   ΣFy = 0   ⇒   a = 0
Two kinds of equilibrium
Equilibrium State Velocity (v) Acceleration (a) Net Force (ΣF) Real-World Example
Static Equilibrium v = 0 (At rest) a = 0 ΣF = 0 A parked car; a book resting on a table.
Dynamic Equilibrium v = constant ≠ 0 a = 0 ΣF = 0 A cruiser at 100 km/h; skydiver at terminal speed.

The key takeaway: constant velocity means zero net force. If an object moves at a steady speed without turning, all the forces on it cancel.

Common mistake

Many students think an object needs a force to keep moving. It does not. A force is needed only to change the motion.

05 Free-body diagrams

The Authentic Free Body Diagram Protocol

A free body diagram (FBD) is a simplified picture of one isolated object. It reveals every external force vector acting on the body:

The 4 Rules of Authentic Free Body Diagrams

  1. Isolate the Object: Model the body as a point mass or a simple box separated from its surroundings.
  2. Draw Vectors Outward: Every force arrow starts on the object and points away, in the direction of the push or pull.
  3. Scale Arrow Length: The length of each arrow must reflect its relative magnitude (equal forces have equal lengths).
  4. Include Forces Acting ON the Body Only: Never draw forces exerted by the object, and never draw velocity or inertia vectors.
Common mistake

Do not draw a "force of motion" or a "force of inertia". Inertia is not a force, and an object does not need a forward force to keep moving.

Problem 2.1: Terminal Velocity of a Skydiver

Written response3 marks

A skydiver with mass 75 kg jumps from an aircraft. After falling for several seconds, she reaches a constant terminal velocity of 54 m/s downward.

A classmate argues: "The skydiver is falling very rapidly at 54 m/s. Therefore, the downward force of gravity must be much larger than upward air drag."

Evaluate this statement using the Evidence → Claim → Reasoning (ECR) framework.

Scoring guide
  • Evidence (1 mark): You say her velocity is constant at 54 m/s downward, so her acceleration is 0 m/s².
  • Claim (1 mark): You say the upward drag equals her weight: Fdrag = Fg = 75 kg × 9.8 m/s² = 735 N.
  • Reasoning (1 mark): You say zero acceleration needs zero net force (ΣF = 0).
  • You say the forces are balanced even though she moves fast.
Model response (Evidence, Claim, Reasoning)
  • Evidence: The skydiver falls at a constant velocity of 54 m/s downward. Her velocity is not changing (Δv = 0), so her acceleration is 0 m/s².
  • Claim: The classmate's statement is incorrect. Upward air resistance equals downward gravity in magnitude (Fdrag = Fg = 735 N).
  • Reasoning: By Newton's First Law, constant velocity requires zero net force (ΣF = 0). If gravity exceeded drag, the skydiver would accelerate. Because speed is constant, the upward and downward forces balance completely.
