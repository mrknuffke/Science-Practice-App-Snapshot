Lesson 1-2 · Motion Graphs & Uniform Acceleration

A number gives you one snapshot. A graph gives you the whole trip.

A falling supply capsule does not move at constant speed. Gravity pulls it down with constant acceleration. In this lesson you read velocity from the slope of a position graph. You find distance from the area under a velocity graph.

Mission

Operation Projectile Precision. ISA Academy, Drop Zone Facility. A number gives you a single snapshot. A graph gives you the entire history of an object's motion at a glance.

An ultrasonic motion detector sends out a sound pulse. It records the exact time the echo takes to bounce back from an object. The sensor software finds position hundreds of times each second and builds a live curve. To an untrained observer, squiggles on a screen look abstract. To a physicist, those lines reveal speed, direction, acceleration, and distance traveled with mathematical certainty.

This lesson builds two graphing relationships: slope (rate of change) and area under the curve (accumulated change).

02 Position-time graphs

Position vs. Time (x-t): The Slope is Velocity

On an x-t graph, position (x) goes on the vertical axis. Elapsed time (t) goes on the horizontal axis.

Recall the mathematical definition of slope: rise over run (Δy / Δx). On an x-t graph:

Slope = Δx (Rise)Δt (Run) = Velocity (v)

This single relationship unlocks the interpretation of every position-time graph:

Common mistake

A flat line on a position-time graph does not mean the object moves at constant speed. It means the object is at rest.

Enrichment

Instantaneous Velocity and Tangent Lines

Extra depth. The rest of the lesson works without it.

When an object accelerates, its position-time graph curves. Because the slope is constantly changing, the object does not have a single velocity.

To find the instantaneous velocity at an exact second, draw a tangent line. A tangent line brushes against the curve at that precise point. The slope of that tangent line equals the velocity at that moment.

03 Velocity-time graphs

Velocity vs. Time (v-t) & Acceleration

On a v-t graph, velocity (v) goes on the vertical axis and time (t) on the horizontal axis. The slope now has a new meaning:

Slope = Δv (Rise)Δt (Run) = Acceleration (a)

If the v-t line is horizontal, the slope is zero (Δv = 0), so acceleration is 0 m/s². If the line tilts upward, the object accelerates forward (+a). If it tilts downward, the acceleration is negative (−a).

The Power of Area under the Curve

Slope is a division (Δy / Δx). The area under the curve is a geometric multiplication: height × width.

Consider the dimensional analysis of multiplying the units on a v-t graph:

(Vertical Unit) × (Horizontal Unit) = (m/s) × s = meters (m) = Displacement (Δx)

The geometric area bounded between the graph line and the horizontal time axis always equals the net displacement:

Common mistake

A falling line on a velocity-time graph does not always mean the object is slowing down. Once the velocity is negative, a falling line means the speed is growing again, in reverse.

Problem 1.4: Drop Zone Data Analysis

Written response4 marks

During an aerial drop test, an autonomous parachute deployment vehicle records its velocity over an 8.0-second interval.

The graph shows:

  • Segment 1 (t = 0 to 4.0 s): Velocity increases linearly from 0 m/s to 16.0 m/s.
  • Segment 2 (t = 4.0 to 8.0 s): The parachute opens, and velocity decreases linearly from 16.0 m/s back down to 4.0 m/s.

A student calculates a displacement of 128 meters by multiplying 16 m/s by 8 s.

Write an ECR argument (Evidence, Claim, Reasoning) that evaluates this calculation and finds the true displacement.

Scoring guide
  • Method (1 mark): You say displacement equals the area under the velocity-time graph.
  • You say multiplying 16 m/s by 8 s counts area that was never traveled.
  • Area 1 (1 mark): You find the triangle in Segment 1: ½ × 4 × 16 = 32.0 m.
  • Area 2 (1 mark): You find the trapezoid in Segment 2: 40.0 m.
  • Total (1 mark): You add the areas to get 72.0 m, with units.
Model response (Evidence, Claim, Reasoning)
  • Evidence: Segment 1 forms a triangle (base = 4.0 s, height = 16.0 m/s). Segment 2 forms a trapezoid (base = 4.0 s, initial height = 16.0 m/s, final height = 4.0 m/s).
  • Claim: The student's calculation is incorrect. The formula (vmax × Δt = 128 m) assumes constant top speed. The true displacement is the total area under both segments, 72.0 meters.
  • Reasoning: Area 1 (triangle): ½ × (4.0 s) × (16.0 m/s) = 32.0 m. Area 2 (rectangle plus triangle): (4.0 s × 4.0 m/s) + ½(4.0 s × 12.0 m/s) = 16.0 m + 24.0 m = 40.0 m. Total Δx = 32.0 m + 40.0 m = 72.0 m. Multiplying peak velocity by total time counts area that was never traveled.
